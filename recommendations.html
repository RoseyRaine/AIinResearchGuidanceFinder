01RecommendationUse AI to Complement, Not Replace, Academic Skills+

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 1, AI tools can be used to support thinking but should not substitute for intellectual effort. GenAI should boost or build upon researchers’ analytical and writing capabilities, not replace critical thinking, creativity, or deep analysis. Researchers must actively engage with any AI outputs, refining and contextualising them according to their specific research frameworks.

Examples of appropriate use:

  • Increasing understanding of research methods or practising those methods using hypothetical scenarios.
  • Increasing understanding of different forms of data analysis or practising analytical methods using synthetic or dummy data. Using AI with synthetic, dummy, public or appropriately licensed non-sensitive data to learn or test an analytical workflow will usually be lower risk, provided the output is independently verified. Use of AI with actual research data requires additional consideration of ethics approval, participant consent, data governance, privacy, tool conditions and methodological validity.
  • Improving writing capabilities: for example, asking GenAI to outline potential chapter structures, then critically refining the output through engagement with the literature.
  • Developing career skills, including drafting or tailoring a CV or cover letter.

Using AI to analyse actual research data - including AI functionality embedded within qualitative or statistical analysis software - requires additional consideration of ethics approval, participant consent, data governance, privacy, tool conditions and methodological validity. See Recommendation 2 and Appendix A for further guidance on assessing whether a proposed use of AI is appropriate.

Honours students are especially encouraged to treat GenAI as a thinking partner, not a writing service. The development of your own analytical and writing voice is itself a key outcome of honours-level research. Students should apply reflective practice to their AI usage to prevent over-reliance and ensure AI enhances rather than replaces intellectual effort.

02RecommendationSafeguard Data Privacy and Security+

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 2, researchers need to understand the rights of data owners and their responsibilities for data input in terms of sensitivity, copyright or intellectual property. GenAI providers publish Terms of Service outlining what is allowed regarding the uploading of copyright-protected materials.

Researchers should be aware that information entered into online AI tools may be stored, processed or retained according to the provider’s terms and privacy settings. Researchers should not upload identifiable, confidential or sensitive research data into public/consumer AI tools.

For example, a researcher exploring a quantitative analysis workflow could use synthetic or dummy data to test the approach before applying it to approved research data. For qualitative research, interview transcripts or other participant material should not be uploaded into a public/consumer AI tool simply because names have been removed; rich qualitative data may still be re-identifiable and may remain subject to ethics, consent and data governance requirements.

The University recommends that copyrighted text, confidential information, or intellectual property should not be entered into an online digital assistance tool without the express permission of the owner. Charles Sturt provides staff and students with access to Microsoft Copilot with Enterprise Data Protection through their University account. This provides additional contractual, privacy and security protections compared with consumer AI services; however, it does not mean that all research data are approved for AI-assisted processing.

A paid subscription, private account or disabled data-sharing setting does not automatically make an AI tool suitable for research data. Researchers should consider the tool’s actual privacy, security, data storage, processing, contractual and training-on-input arrangements, as well as University approval, ethics requirements, participant consent and data governance obligations. See the AI tool checklist in Appendix D. Where available, University-approved or protected tools should be preferred for research-related use, but approval of a tool does not mean that all data types or uses are permitted.

Before using an AI tool, classify the material you plan to enter; when in doubt, treat the material as higher risk and seek advice. The Library’s Can I upload this to an AI tool? decision tool provides a quick first check on copyright and platform terms. These resources do not replace ethics, privacy, confidentiality, contractual or data-governance requirements.

03RecommendationPractise Ethical Use and Integrity+

Where GenAI has directly informed research ideas, text, analysis or argument, this should be acknowledged in sufficient detail to enable the reader to understand the nature and extent of its use.

Researchers are responsible for the integrity, accuracy and originality of their work, including any material produced with the assistance of AI. AI use should be transparent and appropriately acknowledged or disclosed where it has materially contributed to the development, analysis, interpretation or presentation of research. Use of AI must also comply with Charles Sturt University research integrity requirements and any relevant funder, publisher, disciplinary or professional expectations.

Researchers should keep an appropriate record of material AI use throughout the research process to support transparency, reproducibility and later disclosure. See Appendix B for a suggested approach to documenting tools, prompts, outputs, verification and other relevant details.

AI may be used to assist with editing or refining researcher-authored text where the researcher retains control over the substance, meaning, evidence and scholarly argument. Examples include:

  • Asking GenAI to delete unnecessary words and phrases from researcher-authored text to make it more concise without changing its meaning.
  • Asking GenAI to suggest alternative wording in researcher-authored text, with the researcher reviewing and deciding whether to accept the changes.

AI and other digital assistance tools cannot be named as authors. Researchers remain responsible for all content submitted or published in their name. Acknowledgement or disclosure is appropriate and expected where AI materially informs:

  • Refinement of language or editing
  • Brainstorming, conception and design of a project or output
  • Analysis or interpretation of research data
  • Providing initial drafts of the research output or critically revising it
  • AI-assisted literature search, screening, extraction or synthesis (report in methods section)
  • AI-generated images, diagrams, media, code or datasets (label clearly in captions and methods)

The level of detail required will depend on the nature and extent of the AI use, disciplinary expectations, and publisher, funder or institutional requirements.

Researchers using AI to edit or correct text should also consider the Australian Standards for Editing Practice, which indicate that editing should not change the substance of a researcher’s work. When using AI for editing or proofreading, researchers must check that the tool has not changed technical terminology, altered meaning, removed quotation marks, changed citations, paraphrased quoted text or introduced unsupported claims. AI editing should improve expression only and must not change the substance, evidence base or attribution of the work.

Acknowledgement template

I acknowledge the use of [e.g. ChatGPT 4.0 / Google Gemini / Microsoft Copilot] to support aspects of this thesis/paper/research.

I used these tools to [e.g. identify limitations in past research / refine language / draft sections that I subsequently substantially revised / assist with preliminary keyword generation for the literature review].

I did not insert any output generated by these tools verbatim but instead used their output to inspire and develop my own ideas.

04RecommendationConduct Critical Evaluation of AI Outputs+

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 3, researchers are responsible for the careful interpretation and representation of outputs. AI output has the potential to be inaccurate, misleading, obsolete or inappropriate. Researchers should apply a critical lens when using GenAI outputs.

AI tools are not neutral sources of information. Their outputs may reflect training data, system design, interface choices and dominant scholarly patterns, so researchers should evaluate not only whether outputs are accurate, but also how they frame the topic.

Where AI contributes to analysis, classification, coding, screening, extraction or interpretation, researchers should consider whether results are sensitive to prompt wording, model version, settings or repeated runs. Appropriate validation may include testing alternative prompts, repeating the task, documenting variation in outputs and assessing whether substantive conclusions remain stable.

Do not treat an AI tool’s confidence, explanation, self-critique or agreement with the researcher as independent validation. Important outputs should be checked against original evidence, established methods and human expertise. Where a second AI tool is used as part of a checking process, it should supplement rather than replace independent human verification.

05RecommendationUphold Inclusivity and Integrity+

GenAI tools are trained on vast amounts of publicly available text data. As the values of the owners and creators of the data used to train many AI models are based primarily in wealthy, Western nations, perspectives may be limited and, as a result, may contain biases that reflect social inequalities, stereotypes, and discrimination.

For example, if a model has been trained on text that disproportionately portrays women in caregiving roles or minorities in a negative light, the output may reflect these imbalances. GenAI models can often ‘parrot’ back content that may include harmful statements (Bender et al., 2021), including bias or responses that are not deemed ethical or appropriate in society.

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 3, researchers should actively check whether AI outputs:

  • Are geographically, culturally and linguistically narrow in their sources and examples
  • Speak for communities or groups without appropriate authority or evidence
  • Reflect ableist, racist, sexist, colonial, ageist, classist or heteronormative assumptions
  • Are accessible, including alt text, readable structure and plain-language options where appropriate
  • Handle translation, transcription or analysis involving dialects, low-resource languages or culturally specific terms with appropriate care

Recent research points to the ability to counter inherent inclusivity issues through the strategic use of prompts (Mallick et al., 2024) but ensuring such inclusivity requires ongoing discussion and repeated analysis to evaluate whether this is working.

06RecommendationAI in Literature Reviews+

AI tools can be useful in the early scoping stages of a literature review - for example, identifying broad themes, terminology, and research threads in an unfamiliar area. However, AI tools are not substitutes for systematic, transparent and reproducible searching. This is particularly important for systematic reviews, scoping reviews, rapid reviews, integrative reviews, evidence maps and other systematic-like reviews.

Review taskSuitable AI supportWhat must remain human-led
Clarifying review questionGenerating possible PICO, PCC, SPIDER or other frameworks; testing scope; identifying synonyms.Final research question, inclusion criteria and rationale.

SynthesisGenerating possible themes, tables or narrative structures.Interpretation, judgement, critical appraisal and final synthesis.
ReportingDrafting methods language for AI use.Transparent reporting of search methods, databases, dates, tools and limitations.

Levels of AI tool use in systematic-like reviews

For systematic reviews, scoping reviews, rapid reviews, integrative reviews, evidence maps and other systematic-like reviews, researchers should consider not only whether AI use is allowed, but what level of reliance is appropriate for the specific tool, task and review context.

Level of AI useWhat this means
Acceptable for useThe AI tool may be used as part of the research workflow if limitations and potential biases are understood, documented and accounted for.
Human verification requiredThe AI tool may support a task, but the output must be checked by a researcher before being relied on. This may require reading, revising or verifying the full output.
Requires validation in contextThe AI tool may be used only if its performance is evaluated for the specific project, dataset, discipline or review task and found to be adequate.
Exploratory or supplementary use onlyThe AI tool may help with orientation, brainstorming, scoping or identifying possible directions, but it must not replace established research or review methods.
Not acceptable for useThe tool or output is not reliable enough for the proposed task and should not be relied on for research decisions, review findings or conclusions.

This approach is consistent with the Cochrane, Campbell Collaboration, JBI and Collaboration for Environmental Evidence position statement and the RAISE guidance. AI use in evidence synthesis must be justified, transparent, human-led and supported by appropriate validation or verification.

The appropriate level of AI use will vary by task. AI-assisted deduplication, keyword analysis or exploratory scoping may be acceptable where tools are transparent and outputs can be checked. However, AI-generated search strategies, screening decisions, data extraction, critical appraisal and synthesis require higher levels of human verification, validation and caution.

AI tools should only be used where their role is appropriate to the review method and can be documented transparently. Researchers should consider whether the tool has been validated for the specific task and context, supports reproducible methods, protects data appropriately, and could affect the comprehensiveness, accuracy, bias or conclusions of the review. Where these requirements cannot be met, the tool should not be used for review decisions.

AI-generated summaries may assist understanding, but they should not be treated as evidence synthesis. Synthesis requires human methodological judgement, including decisions about how studies are compared, how uncertainty and heterogeneity are handled, and how conclusions are supported by the evidence.

Researchers should be especially cautious with agentic or end-to-end AI platforms that claim to search, screen, extract data and produce review-style outputs from a simple query. These tools may be useful for exploratory scoping, but their outputs should not be used as the basis for clinical, policy, regulatory or other high-stakes decisions unless the methods are transparent, validated and human-led.

Researchers should never cite AI-generated references without locating and checking the original source. Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science and Charles Sturt Library databases should form the backbone of literature searching. Honours students undertaking a literature review for the first time should consult their supervisor and the Charles Sturt Library about appropriate search strategies. The Library provides training on systematic searching and reference management tools such as EndNote.

07RecommendationTranslation and Multilingual Use+

AI translation tools can be valuable for researchers working with multilingual sources or for non-native English speakers refining the expression of their ideas. However, several important considerations apply.

Translating source material

  • AI translations should be treated as a draft to be reviewed and refined, not as a definitive translation. Academic translation of specialised texts requires domain-specific knowledge that AI tools often lack.
  • Always cite the original source in its original language. If you provide your own translation, note this in the citation.
  • Do not input unpublished or confidential material from participants into public AI translation tools without appropriate consent and ethics approval.
  • Be cautious with dialects, low-resource languages and culturally specific terms, as AI tools may produce poor or misleading results.

Refining your own writing in English

  • AI may be used to help non-native English speakers express their ideas more fluently, provided the intellectual content and argument remain entirely your own.
  • Acknowledge AI assistance in language refinement in your acknowledgements section.
  • Be alert to AI altering your meaning or introducing claims you did not intend. Always review AI-refined text carefully against your original draft.

Honours students and HDR candidates who are non-native English speakers are encouraged to speak with their supervisor about appropriate language support options, including Charles Sturt’s academic language support services.

08RecommendationAI-Generated Images and Multimodal Outputs+

AI image generation tools present specific challenges for academic research. Researchers should be aware of the following considerations before using AI-generated images, diagrams, figures or other visual outputs in their work.

Accuracy and scientific integrity

AI image generators are not numerically accurate and may conform to a prompt by theme and visual impression rather than with precision and factual detail. AI-generated images are generally not appropriate for use as figures in academic publications, data visualisations, or scientific diagrams. All quantitative figures must be produced from your actual data using validated tools.

Bias in AI-generated imagery

AI image models are prone to bias due to their training data. Descriptions of professions, geographies or communities may produce stereotyped or unrepresentative imagery. Critically evaluate any AI-generated images for bias before use.

Copyright and intellectual property

The copyright status of AI-generated images remains legally unsettled in Australia and internationally. Do not use AI tools to reproduce or closely imitate the style of a specific identifiable artist without consideration of intellectual property implications. Check tool terms regarding ownership, reuse, confidentiality and publication of outputs before including AI-generated images in publications or theses.

Disclosure

Any AI-generated image used in a thesis, paper, or research output must be clearly identified as such in the figure caption and in the methods or acknowledgements section. Check your target publisher’s author guidelines before submitting, as many publishers now restrict or prohibit AI-generated figures.

09RecommendationOngoing Learning and Adaptation+

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 4, researchers should maintain contemporary knowledge of AI tools, including their capabilities, limitations, risks and evolving ethical and disciplinary expectations.

AI should be used where it is appropriate and proportionate to the research task, rather than by default. Researchers should consider whether a simpler or non-AI approach would achieve the same purpose and avoid unnecessary repeated prompting, large-file processing or other computationally intensive use. Environmental impact should be considered where relevant. See Section 13 for AI literacy in research.
