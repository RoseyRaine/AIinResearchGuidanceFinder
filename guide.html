1. Purpose and Scope#

Artificial intelligence (AI), including generative AI (genAI), can support research by assisting across a wide range of different tasks and workflows to streamline processes. However, using these tools also introduces important challenges regarding research integrity, privacy, copyright, intellectual property, cultural knowledge, human participants, and scholarly standards. This guidance explains how AI may be used responsibly in research while proactively addressing these risks.

This guidance applies to all of the following stakeholders across Charles Sturt University:

  • Academic and professional staff conducting research
  • HDR candidates, including doctoral, professional doctoral and masters by research candidates
  • Honours students undertaking research projects
  • Research assistants, adjuncts, visiting researchers and collaborators where applicable
  • Supervisors, principal investigators, research group leaders and project teams
  • Thesis examiners, reviewers, assessors and panel members
  • Staff providing research support, including library, ethics, research integrity, copyright, data management and graduate research support

This guidance should be read alongside Charles Sturt’s Statement of Principles for the Use of Artificial Intelligence (https://policy.csu.edu.au/document/view-current.php?id=577), which sets out the University’s overarching principles for the ethical and responsible use of AI, including guidance specific to research. Researchers must also comply with other relevant University, national, funder, publisher and discipline-specific requirements. Key related policies, codes and guidance are listed in Appendix F: Related policies, codes and guidance.

This guidance supports decision-making but does not replace formal University policy, ethics approval, contractual obligations, legal advice, funder conditions or publisher requirements.

Given the rapid pace of AI development, this document will be reviewed and updated regularly.

2. Core Principles#

The advice in this document is underpinned by Charles Sturt University’s Statement of Principles for the use of Artificial Intelligence. The four core guidelines are:

  • GenAI technology can be used to support a researcher’s intellectual and scholarly work and not as a substitute for it.
  • Researchers are responsible for what is inputted into GenAI technology and must have the appropriate consent and authorisation, such as copyright or intellectual property, for any content they input.
  • Researchers are responsible for the careful interpretation and representation of outputs generated by GenAI technology (including citation).
  • Researchers should maintain contemporary knowledge of GenAI technology, including its benefits and risks, to ensure the context of its use aligns with The Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research.

The following principles underpin all advice in this document and reflect Charles Sturt’s four Guidelines on the Use of AI Tools in Research.

PrincipleWhat this means
Human accountabilityResearchers remain responsible for all research decisions, claims, data, analysis, outputs and publications. AI cannot take responsibility, provide consent, verify truth or be named as an author.
Research integrityAI must support, not replace, intellectual labour, critical analysis, methodological rigour, creativity, disciplinary judgement or supervisory oversight.
Transparency and declarationMaterial AI use must be documented and declared in a way that enables readers, supervisors, examiners, reviewers and publishers to understand what was used, why, and how outputs were checked.
Privacy, confidentiality and securityResearchers must not enter confidential, identifiable, sensitive, unpublished, culturally restricted, commercial-in-confidence or third-party copyrighted material into AI tools unless authorised and appropriate safeguards are in place.
Consent and ethicsAI use involving participant data, human research data, recordings, transcripts, images, social media content or sensitive datasets must be considered in ethics applications, participant information sheets, consent processes and data management plans.
Copyright and intellectual propertyResearchers must have the right to use, upload, transform or reproduce material, and must check whether AI-generated outputs can be used, published or commercialised.
Accuracy and verificationAI outputs must be independently checked against reliable sources, methods, data and disciplinary expertise. AI-generated references, quotations, legal claims, statistics and facts require particular caution.
Equity, inclusion and cultural safetyAI systems are not neutral. Bias can arise from training data, data collection and classification practices, model design, system objectives and the social and institutional contexts in which AI is developed and used. Researchers must assume that AI outputs can reproduce, amplify or obscure existing biases, stereotypes, exclusions and dominant perspectives. Researchers are responsible for identifying and addressing these effects throughout the research process, including colonial, ableist, racist, sexist, ageist, classist, heteronormative and other forms of bias. Particular care is required where research involves Indigenous knowledges, culturally sensitive research, marginalised or underrepresented communities.
Reproducibility and record keepingAI use that influences research design, methods, analysis, outputs or interpretation should be recorded sufficiently to support review, audit, replication and examination.
Proportionality and sustainabilityResearchers should use AI only where it is appropriate, beneficial and proportionate to the task, and should consider environmental impact and computational intensity where relevant.
Informed use and AI literacyResearchers should maintain sufficient contemporary understanding of the AI tools they use to make informed decisions about their suitability for research. This includes understanding relevant capabilities and limitations, potential bias and omissions, and how information entered into the tool may be stored, processed, accessed, reused or used for system improvement. Researchers should review relevant terms, privacy and data-handling arrangements and seek advice where risks or conditions are unclear.

6. Recommendations for Responsible and Ethical Use#

The following recommendations elaborate on the quick decision guide and provide practical guidance across key areas of research activity. Each recommendation is linked to the Charles Sturt core guidelines. Case studies are included in Appendix E.

Recommendation 1: Use AI to Complement, Not Replace, Academic Skills#

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 1, AI tools can be used to support thinking but should not substitute for intellectual effort. GenAI should boost or build upon researchers’ analytical and writing capabilities, not replace critical thinking, creativity, or deep analysis. Researchers must actively engage with any AI outputs, refining and contextualising them according to their specific research frameworks.

Examples of appropriate use:

  • Increasing understanding of research methods or practising those methods using hypothetical scenarios
  • Increasing understanding of different forms of data analysis or practising analytical methods using synthetic or dummy data. Using AI with synthetic, dummy, public or appropriately licensed non-sensitive data to learn or test an analytical workflow will usually be lower risk, provided the output is independently verified. Use of AI with actual research data requires additional consideration of ethics approval, participant consent, data governance, privacy, tool conditions and methodological validity.
  • Improving writing capabilities: for example, asking GenAI to outline potential chapter structures, then critically refining the output through engagement with the literature
  • Developing career skills, including drafting or tailoring a CV or cover letter

Using AI to analyse actual research data - including AI functionality embedded within qualitative or statistical analysis software - requires additional consideration of ethics approval, participant consent, data governance, privacy, tool conditions and methodological validity. See Recommendation 2 and Appendix A for further guidance on assessing whether a proposed use of AI is appropriate.

Honours students are especially encouraged to treat GenAI as a thinking partner, not a writing service. The development of your own analytical and writing voice is itself a key outcome of honours-level research. Students should apply reflective practice to their AI usage to prevent over-reliance and ensure AI enhances rather than replaces intellectual effort.

Recommendation 2: Safeguard Data Privacy and Security#

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 2, researchers need to understand the rights of data owners and their responsibilities for data input in terms of sensitivity, copyright or intellectual property. GenAI providers publish Terms of Service outlining what is allowed regarding the uploading of copyright-protected materials.

Researchers should be aware that information entered into online AI tools may be stored, processed or retained according to the provider’s terms and privacy settings. Researchers should not upload identifiable, confidential or sensitive research data into public/consumer AI tools.

For example, a researcher exploring a quantitative analysis workflow could use synthetic or dummy data to test the approach before applying it to approved research data. For qualitative research, interview transcripts or other participant material should not be uploaded into a public/consumer AI tool simply because names have been removed; rich qualitative data may still be re-identifiable and may remain subject to ethics, consent and data governance requirements.

The University recommends that copyrighted text, confidential information, or intellectual property should not be entered into an online digital assistance tool without the express permission of the owner. Charles Sturt provides staff and students with access to Microsoft Copilot with Enterprise Data Protection through their University account. This provides additional contractual, privacy and security protections compared with consumer AI services; however, it does not mean that all research data are approved for AI-assisted processing.

A paid subscription, private account or disabled data-sharing setting does not automatically make an AI tool suitable for research data. Researchers should consider the tool’s actual privacy, security, data storage, processing, contractual and training-on-input arrangements, as well as University approval, ethics requirements, participant consent and data governance obligations. See the AI tool checklist in Appendix D. Where available, University-approved or protected tools should be preferred for research-related use, but approval of a tool does not mean that all data types or uses are permitted.

Before using an AI tool, classify the material you plan to enter, when in doubt, treat the material as higher risk and seek advice. The Library’s Can I upload this to an AI tool? decision tool provides a quick first check on copyright and platform terms. For more detailed guidance and the tool, see the Copyright - GenAI Library Guide. These resources do not replace ethics, privacy, confidentiality, contractual or data-governance requirements.

Recommendation 3: Practise Ethical Use and Integrity#

Where GenAI has directly informed research ideas, text, analysis or argument, this should be acknowledged in sufficient detail to enable the reader to understand the nature and extent of its use.

Researchers are responsible for the integrity, accuracy and originality of their work, including any material produced with the assistance of AI. AI use should be transparent and appropriately acknowledged or disclosed where it has materially contributed to the development, analysis, interpretation or presentation of research. Use of AI must also comply with Charles Sturt University research integrity requirements and any relevant funder, publisher, disciplinary or professional expectations.

Researchers should keep an appropriate record of material AI use throughout the research process to support transparency, reproducibility and later disclosure. See Appendix B for a suggested approach to documenting tools, prompts, outputs, verification and other relevant details.

AI may be used to assist with editing or refining researcher-authored text where the researcher retains control over the substance, meaning, evidence and scholarly argument. Examples include:

  • Asking GenAI to delete unnecessary words and phrases from researcher-authored text to make it more concise without changing its meaning.
  • Asking GenAI to suggest alternative wording in researcher-authored text, with the researcher reviewing and deciding whether to accept the changes.

AI and other digital assistance tools cannot be named as authors. Researchers remain responsible for all content submitted or published in their name. Acknowledgement or disclosure is appropriate and expected where AI materially informs:

  • Refinement of language or editing
  • Brainstorming, conception and design of a project or output
  • Analysis or interpretation of research data
  • Providing initial drafts of the research output or critically revising it
  • AI-assisted literature search, screening, extraction or synthesis (report in methods section)
  • AI-generated images, diagrams, media, code or datasets (label clearly in captions and methods)

The level of detail required will depend on the nature and extent of the AI use, disciplinary expectations, and publisher, funder or institutional requirements.

Researchers using AI to edit or correct text should also consider the Australian Standards for Editing Practice, which indicate that editing should not change the substance of a researcher’s work. When using AI for editing or proofreading, researchers must check that the tool has not changed technical terminology, altered meaning, removed quotation marks, changed citations, paraphrased quoted text or introduced unsupported claims. AI editing should improve expression only and must not change the substance, evidence base or attribution of the work.

Recommendation 4: Conduct Critical Evaluation of AI Outputs#

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 3, researchers are responsible for the careful interpretation and representation of outputs. AI output has the potential to be inaccurate, misleading, obsolete or inappropriate. Researchers should apply a critical lens when using GenAI outputs.

AI tools are not neutral sources of information. Their outputs are shaped by training data, data selection and classification, system design, model objectives, moderation and filtering, interface choices and dominant social and scholarly patterns. Researchers should therefore evaluate not only the factual accuracy of outputs but also the assumptions, perspectives, omissions and power relationships embedded in how a topic is framed.

Where AI contributes to analysis, classification, coding, screening, extraction or interpretation, researchers should consider whether results are sensitive to prompt wording, model version, settings or repeated runs. Appropriate validation may include testing alternative prompts, repeating the task, documenting variation in outputs and assessing whether substantive conclusions remain stable.

Do not treat an AI tool’s confidence, explanation, self-critique or agreement with the researcher as independent validation. Important outputs should be checked against original evidence, established methods and human expertise. Where a second AI tool is used as part of a checking process, it should supplement rather than replace independent human verification.

Recommendation 5: Recognise and Address Bias, Equity and Exclusion#

AI systems are not neutral. Bias is embedded in the data, classifications, assumptions, design decisions and social contexts from which AI systems are developed and used. GenAI can reproduce, amplify or obscure stereotypes, discrimination, historical inequalities and dominant cultural or disciplinary perspectives.

Bias is not limited to research specifically about marginalised or underrepresented populations. It can affect any research task in which AI contributes to identifying literature, generating examples, summarising evidence, classifying information, translating language, analysing data, generating images, proposing methods or shaping interpretation.

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 3, researchers are responsible for the careful interpretation and representation of AI outputs and must critically evaluate how bias, exclusion and embedded assumptions may affect those outputs. Researchers must consider whether AI outputs:

  • privilege particular geographical, cultural, linguistic or disciplinary perspectives
  • reproduce racist, sexist, ableist, colonial, ageist, classist, heteronormative or other discriminatory assumptions
  • use deficit framing or stereotypical representations
  • generalise inappropriately across populations, communities or contexts
  • omit or marginalise relevant knowledge systems, methodologies or perspectives
  • make claims about communities or groups without appropriate evidence or authority
  • perform differently across languages, dialects, accents or other characteristics
  • present apparently neutral categories, labels or recommendations that reflect assumptions embedded in the data or system.

Prompting an AI system to provide a wider range of examples or perspectives may be useful as an exploratory technique, but it does not remove structural biases in the model, its training data or its design. Researchers must independently identify appropriate evidence and expertise and remain responsible for the framing, interpretation and representation used in their research.

Responsibility for identifying and addressing AI bias rests with the researcher and research team. It should not be shifted onto members of marginalised or underrepresented communities by expecting them to correct AI-generated stereotypes, omissions or misrepresentation.

Recommendation 6: AI in Literature Reviews#

AI tools can be useful in the early scoping stages of a literature review - for example, identifying broad themes, terminology, and research threads in an unfamiliar area. However, AI tools are not substitutes for systematic, transparent and reproducible searching. This is particularly important for systematic reviews, scoping reviews, rapid reviews, integrative reviews, evidence maps and other systematic-like reviews.

Levels of AI tool use in systematic-like reviews

For systematic reviews, scoping reviews, rapid reviews, integrative reviews, evidence maps and other systematic-like reviews, researchers should consider not only whether AI use is allowed, but what level of reliance is appropriate for the specific tool, task and review context.

Level of AI useWhat this means
Acceptable for useThe AI tool may be used as part of the research workflow if limitations and potential biases are understood, documented and accounted for.
Human verification requiredThe AI tool may support a task, but the output must be checked by a researcher before being relied on. This may require reading, revising or verifying the full output.
Requires validation in contextThe AI tool may be used only if its performance is evaluated for the specific project, dataset, discipline or review task and found to be adequate.
Exploratory or supplementary use onlyThe AI tool may help with orientation, brainstorming, scoping or identifying possible directions, but it must not replace established research or review methods.
Not acceptable for useThe tool or output is not reliable enough for the proposed task and should not be relied on for research decisions, review findings or conclusions.

This approach is consistent with the Cochrane, Campbell Collaboration, JBI and Collaboration for Environmental Evidence position statement and the RAISE guidance. AI use in evidence synthesis must be justified, transparent, human-led and supported by appropriate validation or verification.

The appropriate level of AI use will vary by task. AI-assisted deduplication, keyword analysis or exploratory scoping may be acceptable where tools are transparent and outputs can be checked. However, AI-generated search strategies, screening decisions, data extraction, critical appraisal and synthesis require higher levels of human verification, validation and caution.

AI tools should only be used where their role is appropriate to the review method and can be documented transparently. Researchers should consider whether the tool has been validated for the specific task and context, supports reproducible methods, protects data appropriately, and could affect the comprehensiveness, accuracy, bias or conclusions of the review. Where these requirements cannot be met, the tool should not be used for review decisions.

AI-generated summaries may assist understanding, but they should not be treated as evidence synthesis. Synthesis requires human methodological judgement, including decisions about how studies are compared, how uncertainty and heterogeneity are handled, and how conclusions are supported by the evidence.

Researchers should be especially cautious with agentic or end-to-end AI platforms that claim to search, screen, extract data and produce review-style outputs from a simple query. These tools may be useful for exploratory scoping, but their outputs should not be used as the basis for clinical, policy, regulatory or other high-stakes decisions unless the methods are transparent, validated and human-led.

Researchers should never cite AI-generated references without locating and checking the original source. Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science and Charles Sturt Library databases should form the backbone of literature searching. Honours students undertaking a literature review for the first time should consult their supervisor and the Charles Sturt Library about appropriate search strategies. The Library provides training on systematic searching and reference management tools such as EndNote.

Recommendation 7: Translation and Multilingual Use#

AI translation tools can be valuable for researchers working with multilingual sources or for non-native English speakers refining the expression of their ideas. However, several important considerations apply.

Translating source material

  • AI translations should be treated as a draft to be reviewed and refined, not as a definitive translation. Academic translation of specialised texts requires domain-specific knowledge that AI tools often lack.
  • Always cite the original source in its original language. If you provide your own translation, note this in the citation.
  • Do not input unpublished or confidential material from participants into public AI translation tools without appropriate consent and ethics approval.
  • Be cautious with dialects, low-resource languages and culturally specific terms, as AI tools may produce poor or misleading results.

Refining your own writing in English

  • AI may be used to help non-native English speakers express their ideas more fluently, provided the intellectual content and argument remain entirely your own.
  • Acknowledge AI assistance in language refinement in your acknowledgements section.
  • Be alert to AI altering your meaning or introducing claims you did not intend. Always review AI-refined text carefully against your original draft.

Honours students and HDR candidates who are non-native English speakers are encouraged to speak with their supervisor about appropriate language support options, including Charles Sturt’s academic language support services.

Recommendation 8: AI-Generated Images and Multimodal Outputs#

AI image generation tools present specific challenges for academic research. Researchers should be aware of the following considerations before using AI-generated images, diagrams, figures or other visual outputs in their work.

Accuracy and scientific integrity

AI image generators are not numerically accurate and may conform to a prompt by theme and visual impression rather than with precision and factual detail. AI-generated images are generally not appropriate for use as figures in academic publications, data visualisations, or scientific diagrams. All quantitative figures must be produced from your actual data using validated tools.

Bias in AI-generated imagery

AI image models are prone to bias due to their training data. Descriptions of professions, geographies or communities may produce stereotyped or unrepresentative imagery. Critically evaluate any AI-generated images for bias before use.

Copyright and intellectual property

The copyright status of AI-generated images remains legally unsettled in Australia and internationally. Do not use AI tools to reproduce or closely imitate the style of a specific identifiable artist without consideration of intellectual property implications. Check tool terms regarding ownership, reuse, confidentiality and publication of outputs before including AI-generated images in publications or theses.

Disclosure

Any AI-generated image used in a thesis, paper, or research output must be clearly identified as such in the figure caption and in the methods or acknowledgements section. Check your target publisher’s author guidelines before submitting, as many publishers now restrict or prohibit AI-generated figures.

Recommendation 9: Ongoing Learning and Adaptation#

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 4, researchers should maintain contemporary knowledge of AI tools, including their capabilities, limitations, risks and evolving ethical and disciplinary expectations.

AI should be used where it is appropriate and proportionate to the research task, rather than by default. Researchers should consider whether a simpler or non-AI approach would achieve the same purpose and avoid unnecessary repeated prompting, large-file processing or other computationally intensive use. Environmental impact should be considered where relevant. See Section 13 for AI literacy in research.

7. AI Use Across the Research Lifecycle#

At each stage of the research lifecycle, researchers should consider not only whether AI outputs are accurate, but also how AI may shape the framing, emphasis, methodology, interpretation, scholarly voice and perceived authority of the work.

Researchers should also consider how errors, omissions, bias or hallucinations introduced at one stage of the research process may carry forward into later stages, influencing research questions, methods, analysis, interpretation or conclusions.

AI can be used to simulate a critical conversation, for example by acting as a mock reviewer, audience member or hypothetical respondent to help researchers identify questions, assumptions or areas of uncertainty. However, AI-generated responses are synthetic and cannot substitute for empirical evidence from people, including appropriate participant involvement, piloting, consultation or validation with the intended population.

8. Human Research Ethics, Consent and Participant Data#

Research involving human participants is governed by the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (NHMRC et. al., 2025). If AI will be used to collect, transcribe, translate, process, summarise, classify, code, analyse or store human research data, this should be considered before the research begins. AI use may affect participant consent, data security, data storage location, confidentiality, withdrawal rights, risk assessment and the validity of the approved research method.

This also applies to AI functionality embedded within research software, databases or other platforms. Researchers should not assume that an AI feature is automatically approved simply because the underlying software or service is University-provided or commonly used. Where embedded AI processes human research data or contributes to analysis, researchers should consider whether ethics approval, participant consent, data governance or disclosure requirements apply.

Researchers and students should consider including the following in ethics applications where relevant:

  • Which AI tools or services will be used and for what purpose
  • What data or content will be entered into the tool
  • Whether data will be identifiable, re-identifiable, sensitive, confidential or culturally restricted
  • Where data will be stored or processed, including whether offshore processing may occur
  • Whether the tool provider can retain, review, reuse or train on inputs or outputs
  • How participants will be informed and what they are consenting to
  • How outputs will be verified and by whom
  • How AI use will be recorded in the data management plan
  • What alternatives will be available if AI use is inappropriate or participants do not consent.

AI-generated advice about statistical tests, missing data, imputation, coding, filtering or data transformation must be checked against disciplinary methods guidance and supervisor or statistical advice. Researchers remain responsible for ensuring that analytical decisions are methodologically appropriate, justified and disclosed.

Where AI transcription or captioning tools are used, researchers should consider accuracy, bias and participant risk. Transcription accuracy may vary across accents, dialects, speech patterns, languages and audio quality. Transcripts should be checked before analysis, and participants should be informed where transcription involves AI processing, third-party services, or storage outside approved University systems.

Honours students whose research involves human participants should discuss any planned AI-assisted analysis with their supervisor and check whether their ethics application needs to be updated before proceeding. Participant data gathered under an ethics approval carries specific protections. Even if your ethics application did not anticipate AI-assisted analysis, you must not input identifiable participant information into any public GenAI tool. When in doubt, contact the Human Research Ethics Committee for advice.

Can I upload survey data or interview transcripts into AI tools?
Not into public AI tools. Survey responses, interview transcripts, recordings and other participant data must not be uploaded into public AI tools. Use of a University-approved or protected AI tool does not automatically mean that participant data may be uploaded or processed.
Use of AI with participant data may only be considered where the ethics approval, participant consent, data management plan, data governance requirements and tool conditions permit it. Where AI-assisted processing is permitted, researchers should use an appropriate University-approved, protected or locally hosted/controlled environment that provides suitable privacy, security and data-governance protections. A secure or locally hosted environment reduces some risks but does not remove the need for ethics approval, consent, governance and methodological oversight.
Consent to participate in research or to have data stored does not automatically constitute consent for data to be supplied to an AI system, used to improve an AI service, incorporated into model training or reused for other purposes.
De-identification does not remove all privacy or ethical risk. Detailed qualitative data, combinations of variables, contextual information and information linked with other datasets may allow individuals or communities to be re-identified.
Researchers must also consider what happens to data after it is entered into an AI system. Depending on the tool, contractual arrangements and settings, inputs or outputs may be retained, reviewed, reused for product development, incorporated into model improvement or training processes, or otherwise processed beyond the immediate research task. This may create uses of research data that were not anticipated in the original ethics approval, participant consent or data management plan.
Before using research data with an AI system, researchers must establish whether the provider can retain, reuse or train on the data or outputs, whether these uses can be disabled or contractually excluded, and whether the proposed processing is consistent with participant consent, ethics approval, privacy obligations, data governance requirements and any restrictions on future secondary use.
Where AI-assisted analysis is proposed, researchers should confirm that the intended use is permitted for that specific data, task and tool. Where permitted, an appropriate approved or controlled environment should be used, with advice sought from the supervisor, project lead, ethics committee, privacy/information security and research data management support as appropriate.

Research data stored in OneDrive and using Microsoft 365 Copilot

Charles Sturt recommends OneDrive for storing research data where this is consistent with the approved data management plan. However, storing data in OneDrive does not automatically mean it is approved for AI-assisted processing. Microsoft 365 Copilot works within existing Microsoft 365 permissions, so it may be able to access or use files that the signed-in user already has permission to access, depending on the Copilot experience and University settings.

Researchers must ensure that access permissions are appropriate and that any use of Copilot to summarise, classify, code, analyse or otherwise process research data is consistent with ethics approval, participant consent, data governance requirements, confidentiality obligations and the project data management plan.

9. Indigenous Knowledges, ICIP, Indigenous Data Sovereignty and Cultural Safety#

AI use requires particular care where research involves Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, Indigenous knowledges, Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP), Indigenous Data, language, stories, Country, community-held data, restricted knowledge or culturally sensitive information.

Why particular care is required with ICIP and Indigenous Data

Indigenous knowledges and ICIP are not simply information or content available for unrestricted reuse. ICIP may be communally held, connected to Country, subject to cultural authority and protocols, and restricted according to who may access, interpret, reproduce or share it. First Nations peoples have rights to maintain, control, interpret and protect their ICIP, including rights relating to self-determination, secrecy, cultural integrity, attribution and control over recording and use. Australian copyright or other intellectual property law does not necessarily capture all of these cultural rights and responsibilities (Janke et al., 2025).

AI presents additional risks because material entered into an AI tool or system may be transformed, decontextualised, reproduced, retained or reused, depending on the system and its conditions. AI outputs may also misrepresent Indigenous peoples and knowledges, reproduce colonial assumptions or stereotypes, obscure the authority or provenance of knowledge, or generate material that is culturally inappropriate. The Charles Sturt University ICIP Protocol (Janke et al., 2025) identifies risks including lack of attribution to Traditional Owners, false or misleading information, disregard of cultural protocols and the creation of material that misappropriates or misrepresents ICIP.

Researchers and students must not assume that Indigenous material is appropriate for AI use simply because it is publicly accessible, published, held by the University, legally available or no longer protected by copyright. Legal permission does not necessarily establish cultural permission.

Before using AI with Indigenous peoples, ICIP or Indigenous Data, researchers should establish:

whether the AI use is necessary, appropriate and of benefit

who has cultural authority over the knowledge or data

whether the use is consistent with Indigenous self-determination and Indigenous Data Sovereignty

whether appropriate Free, Prior and Informed Consent has been obtained specifically for the proposed AI use

whether consent needs to be ongoing or renewed if AI use or future data use changes

whether relevant cultural protocols restrict access, interpretation, reproduction, attribution or sharing

whether material includes secret, sacred, gender-restricted or otherwise culturally restricted information

whether the AI provider may retain, reuse, train on or reproduce the material

whether Indigenous contributors or communities will remain involved in interpretation and decisions about outputs

whether attribution, benefit sharing, future access, storage and reuse will be managed

whether the source, provenance and authority of Indigenous information used or generated by the AI can be established, including whether relevant source data were First Nations-led, culturally authoritative, or collected under appropriate consent and governance arrangements.

Researchers should not rely on AI to determine the authority, interpretation or cultural integrity of Indigenous knowledges. Where appropriate, outputs should be reviewed by people with relevant cultural authority rather than relying only on researcher judgement or verification against conventional published sources.

Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) should be treated as an ongoing process rather than a one-off event. Where AI use was not included in the original research design or consent process, researchers should not assume that existing consent covers AI-assisted processing, summarisation, analysis, generation or future reuse. Janke et al.’s (2025) ICIP Protocol specifically identifies ongoing consent, future uses and the continuing involvement of First Nations decision-makers as important considerations.

Attribution should follow the preferences of the relevant contributors or cultural authorities and may require culturally specific forms of attribution, such as clan, language or community identifiers. Requests not to be named should also be respected.

Benefits may be monetary or non-monetary and should be determined with relevant First Nations contributors or communities. Consideration should also be given to access to research outputs and to benefits that arise during or after the project.

Indigenous Data Sovereignty applies across the full data lifecycle, including creation, collection, access, analysis, interpretation, management, dissemination and reuse. AI use should therefore be governed throughout the research process rather than treated solely as a question of whether data may be uploaded to a tool.

Project teams should consult relevant First Nations partners and cultural authorities, Indigenous research governance processes, supervisors, ethics committees and University support services before using AI in these contexts. Agreements and contracts may also be required to protect ICIP and Indigenous Data rights and to place appropriate limits on AI use by collaborators, vendors or other third parties.

10. Funder Requirements#

If your research is funded externally - for example, by the Australian Research Council (ARC), the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC), or an industry partner - the funder’s own policies on the use of AI may apply and may override or supplement university-level guidance. Researchers should always check their funding agreement and the funder’s current published guidance before using GenAI tools in funded research.

ARC and NHMRC: Updated joint policy (effective 28 April 2026)

The ARC and NHMRC released updated and aligned policies on GenAI use in April 2026 (ARC, 2026; NHMRC, 2026). These policies apply to the ARC National Competitive Grants Program and the NHMRC Medical Research Endowment Account grant schemes respectively. The policies preserve academic integrity by ensuring that human judgement and decision-making remain central to grant selection, while recognising that AI tools can support practical tasks such as improving clarity and readability.

Applicants may use AI as a support tool when preparing grant applications. Responsibility for the accuracy, integrity and authorship of submitted content remains entirely with the applicant. Applicants should be aware that anything entered into a public GenAI tool should be treated as potentially public.

Assessors and peer reviewers may now use AI in limited ways: only to assist with grammar, spelling, formatting and readability of their own drafted assessment comments. Assessors must not use AI to form an opinion on the quality of an application, and must not input any application material into a public AI tool such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Perplexity. The ARC describes doing so as a serious breach of confidentiality. Assessors may be asked about their AI use and must be transparent when requested. Where inappropriate use is suspected, the assessment may be removed from the process.

Researchers, applicants and reviewers must not include hidden prompts, invisible text, metadata instructions or other manipulative content intended to influence AI-assisted processing, review or assessment. Reviewers, assessors and staff involved in processing applications should be alert to the possibility that documents may contain hidden prompts designed to influence AI tools.

Other funder requirements

For other funding bodies, industry partners and collaborative grants, key areas where AI requirements may apply include:

  • Disclosure of AI use in grant applications or progress reports
  • Restrictions on using AI tools to process data generated as part of the funded project
  • Intellectual property provisions that may affect ownership of AI-assisted outputs
  • Data sovereignty and storage requirements that may affect which AI tools can be used
  • Confidentiality requirements for peer review of grant applications

HDR candidates and honours students working on funded projects should discuss AI use with their supervisors and ensure that any planned use is consistent with the grant conditions. If in doubt, contact the Research Office for advice.

11. Publishing, Dissemination and Examination#

Publishers and journals

Most major academic publishers now have explicit policies on the use of AI in submitted manuscripts, and these may differ from Charles Sturt’s institutional guidance. Before submitting a manuscript, conference paper, book chapter, grant application, dataset, code or image, researchers must check the relevant publisher, journal, conference, funder and collaborator requirements. Requirements vary and change over time.

  • AI tools cannot be listed as authors of academic publications.
  • Many publishers require disclosure of AI-assisted writing, image generation, data analysis or coding.
  • Some publishers prohibit AI-generated images or require additional permissions and labelling.
  • For ARC and NHMRC grant assessment requirements, including restrictions on AI use by assessors and peer reviewers, see Section 10.
  • Funders may have specific requirements for AI use in applications, assessment, data management, ethics or reporting.

Collaborative projects may have agreements that restrict uploading shared or partner data to third-party tools.

Thesis examiners and assessors

Thesis examiners, assessors and peer reviewers must protect the confidentiality of theses, proposals, manuscripts, datasets, images, examination reports, reviewer comments and other unpublished or confidential material. Such material must not be entered into public/consumer or other non-approved AI tools unless explicitly permitted by the relevant University, publisher, funder or review process and the tool is approved for that purpose.

Where AI use is permitted, it should be limited to support tasks that do not compromise confidentiality or replace independent expert judgement. For example, AI may be used to improve the grammar, spelling or readability of a reviewer’s own drafted comments where this is allowed, but it must not be used to form an opinion, evaluate the quality of the work or make assessment decisions. Any material AI use in preparing examination or review reports should be declared where required by the relevant process.

12. Supervisory Conversations, Milestones and Honours Supervision#

AI use should be discussed early and revisited regularly. The supervisory relationship is an important site for navigating AI use in research. Supervisors and students are encouraged to have an explicit conversation about AI at the outset of the research project, and to revisit this conversation as the project evolves and as AI tools change.

Questions for students to consider

  • What AI tools have I used, and for what purpose?
  • Did AI influence the research question, methods, analysis, interpretation or writing?
  • What records have I kept of my AI use?
  • How did I verify outputs?

Questions for supervisors to consider

  • Is the proposed AI use appropriate for the discipline, project, data type and ethics approval?
  • Does it require disclosure, consent, a protocol amendment or data management change?
  • Have we documented our agreed position on AI use for this project?

Questions for panels or assessors to consider

  • Has the student demonstrated independent knowledge of the work, methods and decisions?
  • Are declarations and records adequate?

Supervisors and assessors should not rely solely on AI-detection tools, which can be unreliable and may produce false positives or false negatives. Where there are concerns, process-oriented conversations, drafts, version histories, AI use logs, oral explanations and methodological questioning are usually more appropriate evidence of engagement.

For honours supervisors specifically

Honours students may have different expectations about AI use based on their experience in coursework subjects. Be explicit about how your expectations for the thesis differ from those in coursework assessment contexts. Remind students that both course-level AI guidance and this research guidance apply to their work, and that where there is uncertainty, they should seek guidance before proceeding.

For HDR candidates, AI use should be addressed at candidature milestones, progress reviews and thesis preparation. Consider adding a prompt to milestone forms asking candidates to record whether and how AI has been used since the previous milestone.

13. Equity, Access and AI Literacy#

Access to AI tools is not equal. Differences in financial resources, hardware, internet connectivity, and digital literacy mean that some researchers have far greater access to genAI capabilities than others. This creates potential inequities within and between research groups, institutions, and countries.

Charles Sturt University is committed to ensuring that all researchers have equitable access to the tools and support they need to engage with AI responsibly. Researchers and students who face barriers to accessing AI tools or AI literacy support are encouraged to:

  • Contact the Charles Sturt Library for guidance on available tools and resources
  • Speak with their supervisor about institutional access options, including the Charles Sturt provided enterprise version of Microsoft Copilot
  • Contact DIT Services for advice on hardware and connectivity support.

Supervisors should be mindful of equity when discussing AI use with their students and should not assume that all students have equivalent access to paid AI subscriptions or high-performance hardware.

Researchers must also consider equity and bias within the research itself. AI systems reflect inequalities and imbalances in their training data, design and development contexts. Many widely used systems disproportionately represent English-language, Western and highly digitised sources and may inadequately represent other populations, languages, cultures, knowledge systems and research traditions.

These biases can influence any AI-supported research task, not only research involving marginalised or underrepresented groups. Researchers should therefore evaluate how AI use affects whose knowledge is visible, whose perspectives are treated as authoritative, what categories are used, what information is omitted and how people or communities are represented.

AI literacy in research

AI literacy in research is not only the ability to use AI tools. It also involves understanding when AI use is appropriate, how AI outputs should be checked, and how AI may shape scholarly judgement, voice and interpretation. Researchers should develop AI literacy across three connected areas:

AI literacy areaWhat this means in research practice
Functional AI literacyKnowing what AI tools can and cannot do, when their use is appropriate or unnecessary, how inputs and outputs are handled, including relevant privacy, storage and data-use arrangements, and how to verify and document AI-assisted work.
Critical AI literacyUnderstanding that AI systems are not neutral and evaluating how training data, design choices, classification systems, omissions, stereotypes and dominant social or scholarly perspectives influence outputs and research decisions.
Methodological AI literacyInvolves understanding how AI use may affect validity and reproducibility, including prompt sensitivity, model and version differences, non-deterministic outputs, appropriate validation, independent verification and transparent reporting of errors or failure modes.
Rhetorical AI literacyMaintaining scholarly voice, disciplinary judgement, authorial agency and transparent attribution when AI contributes to writing, communication, translation, peer review or dissemination.

AI literacy should be understood as part of responsible scholarly practice, not simply as technical skill or tool familiarity.

15. Institutional Responsibilities#

Responsible AI use in research is a shared responsibility. While individual researchers remain accountable for their own research decisions, outputs and declarations, Charles Sturt University has an important role in supporting safe, ethical and effective AI use across the research environment.

The University should continue to support responsible AI use by providing clear guidance, training, advice pathways and access to approved tools where appropriate. This includes support for verifying AI outputs, protecting privacy and confidential information, managing copyright and intellectual property risks, addressing bias, supporting culturally safe research practice, and meeting ethics, data management, publisher and funder requirements.

Charles Sturt should also encourage a culture in which researchers can transparently disclose appropriate AI use without unnecessary stigma or fear of unfair penalty. Guidance should be reviewed regularly as tools, risks, laws, funder expectations and publisher requirements change. Where possible, the University should monitor common AI use cases and emerging issues to identify training needs, improve support, assess tool limitations, and promote proportionate and sustainable AI use.

Where AI tools are provided or recommended for research use, the University should clarify their approved use cases, privacy and security settings, data-handling arrangements, limitations, and any restrictions on the use of confidential, unpublished, sensitive, participant, copyrighted, commercial-in-confidence or culturally restricted material.

Where AI-related research involves First Nations peoples or communities, the University should support continuity of cultural governance, consent and relationship arrangements over time, and coordinate consultation where appropriate to reduce consultation fatigue.

Contracts with AI vendors, collaborators and other third parties should preserve applicable ICIP, Indigenous Data Sovereignty, cultural governance, consent, access and future-use requirements, and should place appropriate limits on AI use where necessary to protect those rights and obligations.

TranslationAI translations are drafts only. Always cite original sources. Acknowledge AI language assistance.
Images and multimodal outputsAI-generated images are generally not suitable for academic publications. Disclose any use. Check publisher policies.
Ongoing learningStay current with AI developments, ethical guidance, and discipline-specific norms.
Human research ethicsCheck your ethics approval before using AI in research with human participants. Seek an amendment if needed.
ICIP, Indigenous Data and cultural governanceDo not use ICIP, Indigenous Data or culturally restricted material with AI without appropriate cultural authority, FPIC, ethics and governance arrangements. Public availability or legal access does not by itself establish cultural permission.
Funder requirementsCheck your grant conditions for AI-related obligations before using GenAI in funded research.
Publishing and examinationCheck journal author guidelines and funder policies for AI disclosure requirements. Examiners must protect confidential material.
Supervisors and studentsHave an explicit conversation about AI use at the start of the project and document agreed positions.
Equity and accessBe alert to access inequities among students and to representation gaps in AI training data.

Appendix A: Researcher AI Self-Check Before Use#

Use this checklist before using an AI tool for a research task, particularly where the task involves research data, unpublished material, human participants, ICIP, Indigenous Data or other sensitive information. Not every question will apply to every use.

Purpose and suitability

  • What task do I want AI to perform, and is AI appropriate and proportionate for this task?
  • What data, content or material will I input, and do I have permission to use it in this way?
  • Is the tool public, commercial, University-approved, locally hosted or otherwise protected?
  • Does the software, database or platform contain embedded AI functionality, and if so, what information is sent to or processed by that feature?
  • Does approval of the overall software or platform also cover the specific AI feature and my intended use?

Data, privacy and permissions

  • Could the input include personal, sensitive, confidential, unpublished, restricted, copyrighted, partner-owned or culturally sensitive information?
  • Does the proposed use align with ethics approval, participant consent, contracts, funder requirements, publisher requirements and University policy?
  • Could the material be re-identified when combined with other information?
  • Can the provider retain, review, reuse or use my inputs or outputs for product improvement or model training?
  • Could data be accessed by third parties or processed offshore?
  • Do participant consent and ethics approval permit these uses, including any downstream or secondary use?
  • Have I reviewed the tool's terms, privacy settings and data-handling arrangements, particularly for unpublished or sensitive research material? (Eacersall et al., 2025).

Bias, accuracy and verification

How will I independently verify the output against appropriate evidence, methods or expertise?

What biases, assumptions, stereotypes, omissions or exclusions could the AI introduce into this task?

What groups, knowledge systems, languages, methodologies or perspectives may be underrepresented or misrepresented?

How will I evaluate these effects independently rather than relying on the AI to assess or correct its own bias?

Could the output include misinformation, fabricated evidence, inappropriate generalisation or misleadingly authoritative claims?

ICIP, Indigenous Data and cultural governance

Does the material contain or relate to Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP), Indigenous Data or culturally restricted knowledge?

Who has cultural authority over the material, knowledge or data?

Are the Indigenous sources or data used by the AI identifiable, appropriately governed and authoritative for this context?

Has Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) been obtained for this specific AI use, where required?

Are there restrictions on access, interpretation, recording, reproduction, attribution, storage, sharing or future use?

Does the proposed AI use support Indigenous self-determination, cultural integrity and appropriate benefit?

Are appropriate Indigenous governance, community permissions and Indigenous Data Sovereignty requirements in place?

Recordkeeping, transparency and advice

Do I need to record this use in an AI use log or research record?

Do I need to declare or acknowledge the use in a thesis, report, manuscript, grant application or presentation?

Who should I ask if I am uncertain about ethics, privacy, copyright, data governance, ICIP, security or acceptable AI use?

Appendix B: AI Use Log Template#

Keep a log where AI materially contributes to research design, methods, analysis, interpretation, writing, outputs or dissemination. Supervisors and project leads may request this log for milestones, thesis submission, publication, ethics review, audit or integrity queries.

The log does not need to capture every minor spelling or grammar check. However, where AI use has influenced research decisions, evidence synthesis, analysis, interpretation or wording, more detailed records are advisable.

Where prompts are used to perform a defined research task, such as screening, data extraction, coding, classification or risk of bias assessment, the prompt should be treated as part of the research method. Researchers should record the prompt or prompt summary, tool and version, date used, input material, output, human verification process, and any changes made after testing.

Where AI performs a defined methodological function, researchers should report not only successful use but also material errors, discrepancies, instability or failure modes identified during testing, and explain how these affected the final method or findings.

Generative AI tools were used to assist with preliminary keyword generation and concept mapping for the literature review. All search strategies were finalised by the researcher, and all citations and claims were verified against bibliographic databases and original sources.

Appendix E: Examples of Responsible Practice#

The following case studies illustrate how the guidance may apply in common research contexts. They are examples only and do not replace ethics, copyright, privacy, funder, publisher or supervisory advice.

Bias applies across these examples

AI systems are not neutral. Bias, omissions and embedded assumptions may affect any AI-supported research task, including literature discovery, writing, methods advice, data analysis, translation, image generation and simulated feedback. Researchers should consider these limitations in every case below rather than treating bias as relevant only to research involving marginalised or underrepresented populations.

Case Study 1: Complementing, Not Replacing Academic Skills
Scenario: Sarah, an HDR candidate studying ethnography, uses a GenAI tool to enhance her understanding of research methods. She asks the AI to generate an example of conducting field research in a hypothetical community to prepare for her fieldwork.
How GenAI helps: The AI tool provides a scenario of observing cultural practices in a community, helping Sarah visualise her approach. She also inputs an overview of her research topic and asks for possible chapter structures. These suggestions are not accepted blindly but are refined according to her critical assessment of existing literature.
Reflection: Sarah enhances her research preparation using AI as a complement, not a replacement. She remains responsible for critical thinking, creativity, and adapting AI insights to her unique research context.
Case Study 2: Safeguarding Data Privacy and Security
Scenario: Alex is researching mental health interventions and wants to use GenAI to explore possible data analysis techniques. They recognise that uploading raw interview transcripts or other identifiable participant data into a public AI tool could breach privacy, ethics or data governance requirements.
How GenAI helps: Alex first uses synthetic or dummy data to test the analytical workflow and to understand how the method works. Before using any actual research data, Alex checks the project’s ethics approval, participant consent, data management plan and the conditions applying to the proposed AI tool. After confirming that the intended use is permitted, Alex uses an appropriate University-approved or protected environment and independently verifies the AI-assisted outputs.
Reflection: Alex distinguishes between using AI to learn or test an analytical approach and using AI to process actual research data. A University-approved tool may provide stronger privacy and security protections, but it does not automatically make all data or uses permissible.
Case Study 3: Practising Ethical Use and Integrity
Scenario: Sophia, a PhD candidate in sociology, used GenAI tools to help refine her literature review, brainstorm new research directions, and identify gaps in existing studies. She realised that some sections of her thesis had been significantly influenced by the ideas generated through the AI tool.
How GenAI helps: Sophia used GenAI to brainstorm research questions by inputting a broad theme and generating specific topics. She also used the tool to refine her thesis language and to identify gaps in existing research. She included a clear acknowledgement statement and cited the tools she used.
Reflection: Sophia’s decision to acknowledge the AI’s influence, without presenting generated content as her own original work, exemplifies how researchers can ethically incorporate GenAI while maintaining integrity and compliance with research guidelines.
Case Study 4: Conducting Critical Evaluation of AI Outputs
Scenario: Mark, an HDR candidate studying social media influence, uses GenAI to gather potential sources for his literature review. He notices that some references provided by the tool are fictitious.
How GenAI helps: Mark uses the AI tool to identify key areas of research but double-checks every reference and fact against academic databases like Google Scholar and Scopus. In one instance, the AI incorrectly cites a journal article, prompting him to find the correct source manually.
Reflection: Mark critically evaluates AI outputs, recognising that not all content is trustworthy. His attention to detail ensures his literature review is based on credible and verified sources.
Case Study 5: Upholding Inclusivity and Integrity
Scenario: Emma is a researcher studying leadership. She uses GenAI to help explore possible themes for part of her literature review. The AI repeatedly associates senior leadership with male executives and draws most examples from large Western corporations.
How GenAI helps: Emma treats this pattern as evidence of the limitations of the AI output rather than as a representation of the literature. She independently develops her search strategy using appropriate scholarly databases and deliberately examines whether relevant research, settings and perspectives are absent from the AI-generated framing.
Reflection: AI systems are shaped by biased and uneven training data and may reproduce dominant social and scholarly assumptions. Emma does not attempt to “fix” the model or rely on marginalised scholars or communities to correct its output. Instead, she recognises the limitations of the AI, evaluates and corrects for biases in how it has framed the topic, and bases her research on independently identified evidence and appropriate disciplinary judgement. Bias should be considered whenever AI contributes to research, not only when the research concerns marginalised populations.
Case Study 6: Literature Reviews
Scenario: Priya, an honours student in public health, used a GenAI tool to get an overview of research on her topic. The tool produced a convincing-looking list of references, several of which turned out not to exist when she searched for them.
How GenAI helps: Priya used the AI-generated overview to identify key themes and terminology in her field. She then conducted her own systematic search in Scopus and PubMed using those terms, verifying each source independently before including it in her review.
Reflection: By using AI to orient herself in the field rather than to source her references, Priya avoided the risk of hallucinated citations while still benefiting from AI as a scoping tool.
Case Study 7: AI-Generated Images
Scenario: James, a PhD candidate in environmental science, used an AI image generator to produce a conceptual diagram of an ecosystem for inclusion in a conference presentation. He later considered whether the same image could be used in his journal article.
How GenAI helps: For the conference presentation, James disclosed the AI-generated origin of the diagram in his slide notes. For the journal article, he checked the journal’s author guidelines, which did not permit AI-generated figures. He recreated the diagram using a vector graphics tool based on his own research data.
Reflection: Context determines what is appropriate. Disclosure and publisher compliance are non-negotiable. Checking publisher-specific policies before submission saves significant revision work later.
Case Study 8: Ongoing Learning and Adaptation
Scenario: John is a communications researcher focusing on digital media trends. As AI tools continue to evolve, John stays updated on the latest advancements in NLP and attends AI-related workshops to integrate these advancements into his work.
How GenAI helps: John uses NLP-based AI tools to analyse sentiment across social media platforms. He frequently adapts his research methodology based on the latest features in AI tools, ensuring his work remains current. He also engages in academic discussions on AI ethics and attends AI-focused conferences to stay informed.
Reflection: John demonstrates a commitment to ongoing learning, continuously improving his research by incorporating the latest AI advancements. This adaptability ensures his research remains relevant in an evolving academic landscape.
Case Study 9: Human Research Ethics
Scenario: Lena, an HDR candidate in education, received ethics approval to conduct interviews with teachers about their classroom practices. After data collection, she considered using an AI tool to assist with thematic coding of the transcripts.
How GenAI helps: Lena reviewed her ethics application and noted that it did not specify AI-assisted analysis. She consulted her supervisor and sought an ethics amendment before proceeding. She confirmed that the proposed AI-assisted analysis, the data type and the selected tool were permitted. Only after receiving the necessary approval did she use an appropriate University-approved environment.
Reflection: Lena’s careful approach protected both her participants and the integrity of her research. Checking ethics obligations before introducing new analytical tools is essential.
Case Study 10: Publishing
Scenario: David, a postdoctoral researcher, used an AI tool to help him refine the language of a manuscript he was preparing for submission to an international journal. He had also used an AI image generator to produce a conceptual figure.
How GenAI helps: David checked the journal’s author guidelines, which required disclosure of AI-assisted language editing in the acknowledgements and prohibited AI-generated figures. He rewrote the figure using a graphics tool and added an appropriate acknowledgement of AI language assistance.
Reflection: Checking publisher-specific policies before submission, not after, saved David from having to revise his manuscript at a later stage.
Case Study 11: AI as a critical research-design coach
Scenario: Amira is developing the methodology for a proposed research project. She has drafted the research question and an initial study design herself. She asks a GenAI tool to act as a critical research-methods reviewer and identify potential weaknesses, assumptions, confounding factors, feasibility issues and questions a reviewer might raise
How GenAI helps: The AI prompts Amira to consider issues such as sampling, inclusion criteria and whether her chosen outcome measures align with the research question. She treats these suggestions as prompts for further investigation rather than methodological advice. She checks relevant methodological literature, discusses important issues with her supervisor and makes the final design decisions herself.
Reflection: AI can be useful as a critical thinking partner or “devil's advocate”, particularly for identifying questions a researcher may not yet have considered. It should not determine the study design or replace methodological expertise, supervision, statistical advice, disciplinary standards or scholarly evidence. Where AI materially influences the research design, the use should be recorded and disclosed where appropriate.
Case Study 12: Testing survey or interview questions with a simulated audience
Scenario: Daniel is developing interview questions for a study involving rural health professionals. Before piloting the questions with people from the target population, he asks a GenAI tool to review the draft questions from the perspective of a hypothetical participant and identify wording that may be unclear, ambiguous, leading, repetitive or difficult to answer.
How GenAI helps: The AI suggests that several questions contain jargon and identifies places where two issues are being asked in the same question. Daniel uses this feedback to revise the wording before undertaking appropriate expert review and/or piloting with people representative of the intended population.
Reflection: AI may be useful for preliminary question testing, readability checks and identifying possible ambiguity, but an AI model cannot be treated as a substitute for the target population. Its responses do not demonstrate how real participants will understand, interpret or respond to a question and should not be reported as participant data or evidence of instrument validity. AI simulations may also reproduce stereotypes or assumptions about the target population and should never be treated as representing that population's actual views. Where the perspectives, language or cultural context of the target population are important, appropriate involvement of real people remains essential.
Case Study 13 Practising a presentation or interview
Scenario: Mei is preparing to present her research at a conference. She asks a GenAI tool to act as a critical audience member. She provides a non-confidential summary of her project and asks it to generate challenging questions that an academic audience might ask. She then practises answering them. A researcher preparing for a confirmation panel, viva, job interview or media interview could use AI in a similar way.
How GenAI helps: The tool generates questions about the rationale for the study, limitations, methods and interpretation of findings. Mei uses these questions to identify areas where her explanation is unclear and practises communicating her research more confidently.
Reflection: Using AI for rehearsal can support communication skills and critical reflection without replacing the researcher's own knowledge or judgement. Researchers should avoid uploading confidential manuscripts, unpublished results, participant information, examination material or other restricted content into public AI tools. AI-generated questions may also reflect assumptions or gaps and should not be treated as a definitive indication of what a real audience will ask.
Case Study 14: From brainstorming to publication
Scenario: Sophia used GenAI early in her project to brainstorm possible research questions and alternative ways of framing the topic. She did not copy AI-generated text into her manuscript. One of the suggestions, however, substantially influenced the research question she ultimately pursued. Two years later, she is preparing the research for publication and wonders whether the earlier AI use still matters.
How GenAI helps: Sophia reviews her AI-use records and discusses the use with her co-authors. Because the AI materially influenced the conception and design of the research, she checks the target journal's requirements and includes an appropriate disclosure where required.
Reflection: Whether AI-generated wording appears in the final publication is not the only consideration. AI use may still be material where it meaningfully shaped the research question, design, method, analysis or interpretation. Researchers should therefore record significant AI use when it occurs rather than trying to reconstruct it at publication.
Case Study 15: AI, ICIP and Indigenous Data
Adapted from Janke et al., (2025) Charles Sturt University ICIP Protocol.
Scenario: Maya is an HDR candidate undertaking research with a First Nations community. Her interviews with Elders include cultural knowledge and community narratives. She considers uploading the transcripts to an AI tool to generate summaries and preliminary themes.
What Maya considers: Although the tool could reduce transcription and analysis time, Maya recognises that ethics approval to conduct the interviews does not automatically permit the transcripts or ICIP to be processed by AI. She checks whether AI use was included in the approved research design and consent process; whether the relevant Elders and community have given Free, Prior and Informed Consent for this particular use; whether the tool can retain, reuse or train on the material; and whether appropriate cultural authority and Indigenous Data governance arrangements are in place.
She also considers whether AI-generated summaries could alter, decontextualise or misrepresent the meaning of Elders' contributions. She discusses the proposal with the relevant community representatives, her supervisor and appropriate Indigenous research and ethics governance processes before proceeding.
Reflection: Public availability, research access or ethics approval for data collection does not automatically authorise AI processing of ICIP or Indigenous Data. Indigenous peoples must retain appropriate authority over how their knowledges and data are accessed, interpreted, represented and reused. Where AI use is permitted, ongoing consent, cultural authority, data governance, attribution, future use and appropriate review of interpretations should be built into the research process.
