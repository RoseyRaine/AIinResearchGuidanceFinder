Charles Sturt University

Advice Relating to the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Research

For HDR Candidates, Honours Students, Research-Active Staff, Supervisors, Examiners and Research Support Staff

Updated 2026 | Review annually

1. Purpose and Scope#

Artificial intelligence (AI), including generative AI (genAI), can support research by assisting with drafting, summarising, coding, data analysis, translation, brainstorming, literature discovery, teaching, administration and communication. However, AI tools can also produce inaccurate, biased, fabricated, confidential, infringing or non-reproducible outputs. This guidance explains how AI may be used responsibly in research while protecting research integrity, privacy, copyright, intellectual property, cultural knowledge, human participants and scholarly standards.

This guidance applies to all of the following:

  • Academic and professional staff conducting or supporting research
  • HDR candidates, including doctoral and masters by research candidates
  • Honours students undertaking research projects
  • Research assistants, adjuncts, visiting researchers and collaborators where applicable
  • Supervisors, principal investigators, research group leaders and project teams
  • Thesis examiners, reviewers, assessors and panel members
  • Staff providing research support, including library, ethics, research integrity, copyright, data management and graduate research support

This document should be read alongside the Charles Sturt’s Statement of Principles for the Use of Artificial Intelligence (https://policy.csu.edu.au/document/view-current.php?id=577), the Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research, human research ethics requirements, privacy and information security requirements, copyright and intellectual property requirements, records management obligations, funding-body conditions, publisher policies, discipline-specific standards and Indigenous research governance protocols.

This guidance supports decision-making but does not replace formal University policy, ethics approval, contractual obligations, legal advice, funder conditions or publisher requirements.

Given the rapid pace of AI development, this document will be reviewed and updated regularly. Users are encouraged to check for the most recent version throughout their candidature or employment.

2. Core Principles#

The advice in this document is underpinned by Charles Sturt University’s Statement of Principles for the use of Artificial Intelligence. The four core guidelines are:

  • GenAI technology can be used to support a researcher’s intellectual and scholarly work and not as a substitute for it.
  • Researchers are responsible for what is inputted into GenAI technology and must have the appropriate consent and authorisation, such as copyright or intellectual property, for any content they input.
  • Researchers are responsible for the careful interpretation and representation of outputs generated by GenAI technology (including citation).
  • Researchers should maintain contemporary knowledge of GenAI technology, including its benefits and risks, to ensure the context of its use aligns with The Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research.

The following principles underpin all advice in this document and reflect Charles Sturt’s four Guidelines on the Use of AI Tools in Research.

PrincipleWhat this means
Human accountabilityResearchers remain responsible for all research decisions, claims, data, analysis, outputs and publications. AI cannot take responsibility, provide consent, verify truth or be named as an author.
Research integrityAI must support, not replace, intellectual labour, critical analysis, methodological rigour, creativity, disciplinary judgement or supervisory oversight.
Transparency and declarationMaterial AI use must be documented and declared in a way that enables readers, supervisors, examiners, reviewers and publishers to understand what was used, why, and how outputs were checked.
Privacy, confidentiality and securityResearchers must not enter confidential, identifiable, sensitive, unpublished, culturally restricted, commercial-in-confidence or third-party copyrighted material into AI tools unless authorised and appropriate safeguards are in place.
Consent and ethicsAI use involving participant data, human research data, recordings, transcripts, images, social media content or sensitive datasets must be considered in ethics applications, participant information sheets, consent processes and data management plans.
Copyright and intellectual propertyResearchers must have the right to use, upload, transform or reproduce material, and must check whether AI-generated outputs can be used, published or commercialised.
Accuracy and verificationAI outputs must be independently checked against reliable sources, methods, data and disciplinary expertise. AI-generated references, quotations, legal claims, statistics and facts require particular caution.
Equity, inclusion and cultural safetyResearchers must identify and mitigate bias, stereotyping, exclusion, colonial assumptions and misrepresentation, especially in relation to Indigenous knowledges, culturally sensitive research and underrepresented communities.
Reproducibility and record keepingAI use that influences research design, methods, analysis, outputs or interpretation should be recorded sufficiently to support review, audit, replication and examination.
Proportionality and sustainabilityResearchers should use AI only where it is appropriate, beneficial and proportionate to the task, and should consider environmental impact and computational intensity where relevant.

3. Definitions#

TermMeaning in this guidance
AI toolAny software or system that uses artificial intelligence, machine learning, large language models, generative models, automated classification, prediction, summarisation, translation or analysis.
Generative AI (GenAI)AI tools that create or transform text, code, images, audio, video, data, summaries, analyses or other outputs in response to prompts or inputs.
Public or open AI toolA tool available publicly or commercially where inputs may be stored, reviewed, processed offshore, used for product improvement or used in ways outside University control.
University-approved or protected AI toolA tool made available or approved by the University with defined privacy, security, data handling and contractual protections. Currently this includes Microsoft Copilot with Enterprise Data Protection, accessible via Charles Sturt browser and app. Approval may be limited to specific use cases.
Material AI useAI use that meaningfully shapes, influences or contributes to research questions, design, methods, data collection, literature review processes, analysis, interpretation, writing, outputs, publications, funding applications or examination. This is similar to what some international guidance describes as “substantial AI use”. Basic spelling, grammar, formatting or minor readability support in researcher-authored text is not usually considered material use, unless it changes the meaning, argument, analysis or claims.
Low-risk AI useMinor support such as grammar checking, formatting, plain-language rephrasing of researcher-authored text, or learning about general concepts, where no confidential, sensitive or unpublished material is exposed and the output does not materially shape research claims.

6. Recommendations for Responsible and Ethical Use#

The following recommendations elaborate on the quick decision guide and provide practical guidance across key areas of research activity. Each recommendation is linked to the Charles Sturt core guidelines. Case studies are included in Appendix F.

Recommendation 1: Use AI to Complement, Not Replace, Academic Skills#

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 1, AI tools can be used to support thinking but should not substitute for intellectual effort. GenAI should boost or build upon researchers’ analytical and writing capabilities, not replace critical thinking, creativity, or deep analysis. Researchers must actively engage with any AI outputs, refining and contextualising them according to their specific research frameworks.

Examples of appropriate use:

  • Increasing understanding of research methods or practising those methods using hypothetical scenarios
  • Assisting with understanding different forms of data analysis - using synthetic or de-identified data only
  • Improving writing capabilities: for example, asking GenAI to outline potential chapter structures, then critically refining the output through engagement with the literature
  • Developing career skills, including drafting or tailoring a CV or cover letter

Honours students are especially encouraged to treat GenAI as a thinking partner, not a writing service. The development of your own analytical and writing voice is itself a key outcome of honours-level research. Students should apply reflective practice to their AI usage to prevent over-reliance and ensure AI enhances rather than replaces intellectual effort.

Recommendation 2: Safeguard Data Privacy and Security#

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 2, researchers need to understand the rights of data owners and their responsibilities for data input in terms of sensitivity, copyright or intellectual property. GenAI providers publish Terms of Service outlining what is allowed regarding the uploading of copyright-protected materials.

Researchers should be aware that when using online tools, text provided as inputs is offered without copyright or other protection. Tools such as ChatGPT and Copilot by default retain the prompts that are entered and may do so even if requested otherwise. Researchers should avoid uploading identifiable data into GenAI tools. For example, a researcher wanting to identify themes from interview transcripts could upload a de-identified set of values with dummy headers, or use synthetic data for initial ‘tests’ of an approach.

The University recommends that copyrighted text, confidential information, or intellectual property should not be entered into an online digital assistance tool without the express permission of the owner. Charles Sturt provides students and staff with ‘local model’ browser and app access to Microsoft’s enterprise version of Copilot with Enterprise Data Protection, which allows reading of prompts but does not use them as training data for future tools.

Honours students should note that participant data gathered under an ethics approval carries specific protections. Even if your ethics application did not anticipate AI-assisted analysis, you must not input identifiable participant information into any public GenAI tool. Discuss any planned AI-assisted analysis with your supervisor before proceeding.

Recommendation 3: Practise Ethical Use and Integrity#

Where GenAI has directly informed research ideas, text or argument, this should be acknowledged in sufficient detail to enable the reader to gauge the extent of use. Unacknowledged use of digital assistance tools to generate or alter text where authors imply it is their own work may constitute a breach of the Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research 2018.

Under Australian Consumer Law (section 18), individuals may not represent work as their own if it was generated by another person or entity, including AI, as this is regarded as misleading conduct. Use of AI tools also must comply with Charles Sturt research integrity guidelines.

A researcher may insert the output of GenAI into chapters, papers, or thesis if it is primarily derived from their own words. Examples include:

  • Asking GenAI to delete unnecessary words and phrases from a paragraph authored by the user, making the output more concise while remaining the candidate’s own work
  • Asking GenAI to identify more suitable words in a paragraph authored by the user, leaving the output still largely derived from the candidate’s own words

Digital assistance tools cannot be named as authors. Acknowledgement is both appropriate and expected when AI informs any of the following:

  • Refinement of language or editing
  • Brainstorming, conception and design of a project or output
  • Analysis or interpretation of research data
  • Providing initial drafts of the research output or critically revising it
  • AI-assisted literature search, screening, extraction or synthesis (report in methods section)
  • AI-generated images, diagrams, media, code or datasets (label clearly in captions and methods)

Researchers using AI to correct text should also be aware that this may engage the Australian Standards for Editing Practice, which implies that editors should not change the substance of text that researchers write. When using AI for editing or proofreading, researchers must check that the tool has not changed technical terminology, altered meaning, removed quotation marks, changed citations, paraphrased quoted text, or introduced unsupported claims. AI editing should improve expression only and must not change the substance, evidence base or attribution of the work.

Acknowledgement template#

Template: Acknowledging GenAI Use I acknowledge the use of [e.g. ChatGPT 4.0 / Google Gemini / Microsoft Copilot] to support aspects of this thesis/paper/research. I used these tools to [e.g. identify limitations in past research / refine language / draft sections that I subsequently substantially revised / assist with preliminary keyword generation for the literature review]. I did not insert any output generated by these tools verbatim but instead used their output to inspire and develop my own ideas.

Citation examples#

Within the written text, outputs of AI tools must be cited, and these sources should appear in the Reference List. The appropriate style depends on the required citation format for your thesis, paper, or report.

Recommendation 4: Conduct Critical Evaluation of AI Outputs#

In line with Charles Sturt Guideline 3, researchers are responsible for the careful interpretation and representation of outputs. AI output has the potential to be inaccurate, misleading, obsolete or inappropriate. Researchers should apply a critical lens when using GenAI outputs.

AI tools are not neutral sources of information. Their outputs may reflect training data, system design, interface choices and dominant scholarly patterns, so researchers should evaluate not only whether outputs are accurate, but also how they frame the topic.

The following table summarises key risks and how to mitigate them:

RiskExamplesMitigation
Fabricated sourcesNon-existent articles, wrong DOIs, false quotations or invented page numbers.Locate original sources in databases or publisher sites before citing. Never cite an AI-generated reference without independently verifying it.
Inaccurate methods adviceIncorrect statistical test, coding approach, methodological terminology or review method.Check against methods literature, supervisor advice and discipline standards.
Bias and exclusionWestern-centric sources, stereotypes, missing underrepresented groups, deficit framing.Prompt for diversity, but also verify with inclusive sources and disciplinary expertise.
AI outputs appear neutral or authoritativeAI may present information, methods, examples or writing styles as if they are standard or objective, when they reflect training data, system design, interface choices or dominant scholarly patterns. This may influence what appears relevant, authoritative, standard or persuasive.Treat AI outputs as shaped, provisional and requiring review. Check whether the output narrows the topic, privileges dominant perspectives, omits alternative views, flattens disciplinary nuance, or frames one approach as more authoritative than the evidence supports.
OverconfidenceAI presents uncertain, contested or preliminary claims as definitive.Ask the tool for uncertainty and limitations, but verify independently regardless.
Predatory or flawed publicationsAI may recommend sources from journals that have bypassed rigorous peer review.Check all sources against Scopus, Web of Science or equivalent quality databases.

Check disciplinary sources, primary materials and alternative perspectives. Be especially cautious in fields involving conflict, genocide, trauma, politics, marginalised communities or contested histories.

Recommendation 5: Uphold Inclusivity and Integrity#

GenAI tools are trained on vast amounts of publicly available text data. As the values of the owners and creators of the data used to train many AI models are based primarily in wealthy, Western nations, perspectives may be limited and, as a result, may contain biases that reflect social inequalities, stereotypes, and discrimination.

For example, if a model has been trained on text that disproportionately portrays women in caregiving roles or minorities in a negative light, the output may reflect these imbalances. GenAI models can often ‘parrot’ back content that may include harmful statements (Bender et al., 2021), including bias or responses that are not deemed ethical or appropriate in society.

Researchers should actively check whether AI outputs:

  • Are geographically, culturally and linguistically narrow in their sources and examples
  • Speak for communities or groups without appropriate authority or evidence
  • Reflect ableist, racist, sexist, colonial, ageist, classist or heteronormative assumptions
  • Are accessible, including alt text, readable structure and plain-language options where appropriate
  • Handle translation, transcription or analysis involving dialects, low-resource languages or culturally specific terms with appropriate care

Recent research points to the ability to counter inherent inclusivity issues through the strategic use of prompts (Mallick et al., 2024), but ensuring such inclusivity requires ongoing discussion and repeated analysis to evaluate whether this is working.

Recommendation 6: AI in Literature Reviews#

AI tools can be useful in the early scoping stages of a literature review - for example, identifying broad themes, terminology, and research threads in an unfamiliar area. However, AI tools are not substitutes for systematic, transparent and reproducible searching. This is particularly important for systematic reviews, scoping reviews, rapid reviews, integrative reviews, evidence maps and other systematic-like reviews.

Review taskSuitable AI supportWhat must remain human-led
Clarifying review questionGenerating possible PICO, PCC, SPIDER or other frameworks; testing scope; identifying synonyms.Final research question, inclusion criteria and rationale.
Search strategy developmentSuggesting keywords, subject headings, spelling variants and related concepts.Database selection, final search syntax, peer review of search strategy and documentation.
Citation discoveryFinding seed papers, related papers, citation networks or topic clusters.Verification in authoritative databases and inclusion decisions.
ScreeningPrioritising records, highlighting potentially relevant abstracts or checking consistency.Final include/exclude decisions and conflict resolution.
Data extractionCreating draft extraction fields or summarising study details.Checking all extracted data against the source article.
SynthesisGenerating possible themes, tables or narrative structures.Interpretation, judgement, critical appraisal and final synthesis.
ReportingDrafting methods language for AI use.Transparent reporting of search methods, databases, dates, tools and limitations.

Levels of AI tool use in systematic-like reviews#

For systematic reviews, scoping reviews, rapid reviews, integrative reviews, evidence maps and other systematic-like reviews, researchers should consider not only whether AI use is allowed, but what level of reliance is appropriate for the specific tool, task and review context.

Level of AI useWhat this means
Acceptable for useThe AI tool may be used as part of the research workflow if limitations and potential biases are understood, documented and accounted for.
Human verification requiredThe AI tool may support a task, but the output must be checked by a researcher before being relied on. This may require reading, revising or verifying the full output.
Requires validation in contextThe AI tool may be used only if its performance is evaluated for the specific project, dataset, discipline or review task and found to be adequate.
Exploratory or supplementary use onlyThe AI tool may help with orientation, brainstorming, scoping or identifying possible directions, but it must not replace established research or review methods.
Not acceptable for useThe tool or output is not reliable enough for the proposed task and should not be relied on for research decisions, review findings or conclusions.

This approach is consistent with the Cochrane, Campbell Collaboration, JBI and Collaboration for Environmental Evidence position statement and the RAISE guidance. AI use in evidence synthesis must be justified, transparent, human-led and supported by appropriate validation or verification.

The appropriate level of AI use will vary by task. AI-assisted deduplication, keyword analysis or exploratory scoping may be acceptable where tools are transparent and outputs can be checked. However, AI-generated search strategies, screening decisions, data extraction, critical appraisal and synthesis require higher levels of human verification, validation and caution.

AI tools should only be used where their role is appropriate to the review method and can be documented transparently. Researchers should consider whether the tool has been validated for the specific task and context, supports reproducible methods, protects data appropriately, and could affect the comprehensiveness, accuracy, bias or conclusions of the review. Where these requirements cannot be met, the tool should not be used for review decisions.

AI-generated summaries may assist understanding, but they should not be treated as evidence synthesis. Synthesis requires human methodological judgement, including decisions about how studies are compared, how uncertainty and heterogeneity are handled, and how conclusions are supported by the evidence.

Researchers should be especially cautious with agentic or end-to-end AI platforms that claim to search, screen, extract data and produce review-style outputs from a simple query. These tools may be useful for exploratory scoping, but their outputs should not be used as the basis for clinical, policy, regulatory or other high-stakes decisions unless the methods are transparent, validated and human-led.

Researchers should never cite AI-generated references without locating and checking the original source. Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science and Charles Sturt Library databases should form the backbone of literature searching. Honours students undertaking a literature review for the first time should consult their supervisor and the Charles Sturt Library about appropriate search strategies. The Library provides training on systematic searching and reference management tools such as Endnote.

Recommendation 7: Translation and Multilingual Use#

AI translation tools can be valuable for researchers working with multilingual sources or for non-native English speakers refining the expression of their ideas. However, several important considerations apply.

Translating source material#

  • AI translations should be treated as a draft to be reviewed and refined, not as a definitive translation. Academic translation of specialised texts requires domain-specific knowledge that AI tools often lack.
  • Always cite the original source in its original language. If you provide your own translation, note this in the citation.
  • Do not input unpublished or confidential material from participants into public AI translation tools without appropriate consent and ethics approval.
  • Be cautious with dialects, low-resource languages and culturally specific terms, as AI tools may produce poor or misleading results.

Refining your own writing in English#

  • AI may be used to help non-native English speakers express their ideas more fluently, provided the intellectual content and argument remain entirely your own.
  • Acknowledge AI assistance in language refinement in your acknowledgements section.
  • Be alert to AI altering your meaning or introducing claims you did not intend. Always review AI-refined text carefully against your original draft.

Honours students and HDR candidates who are non-native English speakers are encouraged to speak with their supervisor about appropriate language support options, including Charles Sturt’s academic language support services.

Recommendation 8: AI-Generated Images and Multimodal Outputs#

AI image generation tools present specific challenges for academic research. Researchers should be aware of the following considerations before using AI-generated images, diagrams, figures or other visual outputs in their work.

Accuracy and scientific integrity#

AI image generators are not numerically accurate and may conform to a prompt by theme and visual impression rather than with precision and factual detail. AI-generated images are generally not appropriate for use as figures in academic publications, data visualisations, or scientific diagrams. All quantitative figures must be produced from your actual data using validated tools.

Bias in AI-generated imagery#

AI image models are prone to bias due to their training data. Descriptions of professions, geographies or communities may produce stereotyped or unrepresentative imagery. Critically evaluate any AI-generated images for bias before use.

Copyright and intellectual property#

The copyright status of AI-generated images remains legally unsettled in Australia and internationally. Do not use AI tools to reproduce or closely imitate the style of a specific identifiable artist without consideration of intellectual property implications. Check tool terms regarding ownership, reuse, confidentiality and publication of outputs before including AI-generated images in publications or theses.

Disclosure#

Any AI-generated image used in a thesis, paper, or research output must be clearly identified as such in the figure caption and in the methods or acknowledgements section. Check your target publisher’s author guidelines before submitting, as many publishers now restrict or prohibit AI-generated figures.

Recommendation 9: Ongoing Learning and Adaptation#

As per Charles Sturt’s Guideline 4, ongoing learning and adaptation is required to ensure interactive engagement, higher-order thinking, and human accountability for the accuracy and evolving impact of AI-generated content.

Researchers should continuously learn about advancements in GenAI, including understanding its potential and limitations. This involves staying updated on new features, ethical guidelines, and how AI can be tailored to the specific disciplinary context. Staying in touch with AI ethics discussions, attending conferences, and integrating new methods into research processes ensures adaptation as AI technology evolves.

Researchers should also consider the environmental impact of AI use as part of responsible research practice. AI should be used where it is appropriate and proportionate to the task, rather than as a default for all research activity. Researchers should consider whether a simpler tool or non-AI approach would achieve the same purpose, avoid unnecessary repeated prompting or large file processing, and use clear, well-planned prompts to reduce avoidable processing. This is particularly important when using computationally intensive tools for large-scale text, image, audio, video or data analysis.

7. AI Use Across the Research Lifecycle#

At each stage of the research lifecycle, researchers should consider not only whether AI outputs are accurate, but also how AI may shape the framing, emphasis, methodology, interpretation, scholarly voice and perceived authority of the work.

Researchers should also consider how errors, omissions, bias or hallucinations introduced at one stage of the research process may carry forward into later stages, influencing research questions, methods, analysis, interpretation or conclusions.

8. Human Research Ethics, Consent and Participant Data#

Research involving human participants is governed by the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (NHMRC, 2018). If AI will be used to collect, transcribe, translate, process, summarise, classify, code, analyse or store human research data, this should be considered before the research begins. AI use may affect participant consent, data security, data storage location, confidentiality, withdrawal rights, risk assessment and the validity of the approved research method.

Researchers and students should consider including the following in ethics applications where relevant:

  • Which AI tools or services will be used and for what purpose
  • What data or content will be entered into the tool
  • Whether data will be identifiable, re-identifiable, sensitive, confidential or culturally restricted
  • Where data will be stored or processed, including whether offshore processing may occur
  • Whether the tool provider can retain, review, reuse or train on inputs or outputs
  • How participants will be informed and what they are consenting to
  • How outputs will be verified and by whom
  • How AI use will be recorded in the data management plan
  • What alternatives will be available if AI use is inappropriate or participants do not consent

AI-generated advice about statistical tests, missing data, imputation, coding, filtering or data transformation must be checked against disciplinary methods guidance and supervisor or statistical advice. Researchers remain responsible for ensuring that analytical decisions are methodologically appropriate, justified and disclosed.

Where AI transcription or captioning tools are used, researchers should consider accuracy, bias and participant risk. Transcription accuracy may vary across accents, dialects, speech patterns, languages and audio quality. Transcripts should be checked before analysis, and participants should be informed where transcription involves AI processing, third-party services, or storage outside approved University systems.

Honours students whose research involves human participants should discuss any planned AI-assisted analysis with their supervisor and check whether their ethics application needs to be updated before proceeding. When in doubt, contact the Human Research Ethics Committee for advice.

Can I upload survey data or interview transcripts into AI tools? Not into public AI tools. Survey responses, interview transcripts, recordings and other participant data must not be uploaded into public AI tools. Use of AI with participant data may only be considered where the ethics approval, participant consent, data management plan, data governance requirements and tool conditions permit it. De-identified data still requires caution because it may remain re-identifiable, especially qualitative data. Where AI-assisted analysis is proposed, researchers should use an approved secure tool and seek advice from their supervisor, project lead, ethics committee, privacy/information security and research data management support as appropriate.

Research data stored in OneDrive and using Microsoft 365 Copilot#

Charles Sturt recommends OneDrive for storing research data where this is consistent with the approved data management plan. However, storing data in OneDrive does not automatically mean it is approved for AI-assisted processing. Microsoft 365 Copilot works within existing Microsoft 365 permissions, so it may be able to access or use files that the signed-in user already has permission to access, depending on the Copilot experience and University settings.

Researchers must ensure that access permissions are appropriate and that any use of Copilot to summarise, classify, code, analyse or otherwise process research data is consistent with ethics approval, participant consent, data governance requirements, confidentiality obligations and the project data management plan.

9. Indigenous Knowledges, Cultural Safety and Culturally Sensitive Research#

AI use requires particular care where research involves Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, Indigenous communities, cultural knowledge, language, stories, Country, community-held data, restricted knowledge or culturally sensitive information. Public AI tools may misrepresent, appropriate, decontextualise or expose cultural knowledge, and may embed colonial assumptions or harmful stereotypes.

Researchers and students must not enter Indigenous knowledges, community data, cultural material, restricted information, unpublished community narratives or culturally sensitive data into AI tools unless this has been explicitly approved through the relevant ethics, cultural governance, community permission, data sovereignty and University processes.

Project teams should consult supervisors, Indigenous research governance processes, ethics committees and community partners before using AI in these contexts. AI outputs about Indigenous peoples or knowledges must be checked carefully for accuracy, authority, cultural safety and representation.

10. Funder Requirements#

If your research is funded externally - for example, by the Australian Research Council (ARC), the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC), or an industry partner - the funder’s own policies on the use of AI may apply and may override or supplement university-level guidance. Researchers should always check their funding agreement and the funder’s current published guidance before using GenAI tools in funded research.

ARC and NHMRC: Updated joint policy (effective 28 April 2026)#

The ARC and NHMRC released updated and aligned policies on GenAI use in April 2026 (ARC, 2026; NHMRC, 2026). These policies apply to the ARC National Competitive Grants Program and the NHMRC Medical Research Endowment Account grant schemes respectively. The policies preserve academic integrity by ensuring that human judgement and decision-making remain central to grant selection, while recognising that AI tools can support practical tasks such as improving clarity and readability.

Applicants may use AI as a support tool when preparing grant applications. Responsibility for the accuracy, integrity and authorship of submitted content remains entirely with the applicant. Applicants should be aware that anything entered into a public GenAI tool should be treated as potentially public.

Assessors and peer reviewers may now use AI in limited ways: only to assist with grammar, spelling, formatting and readability of their own drafted assessment comments. Assessors must not use AI to form an opinion on the quality of an application, and must not input any application material into a public AI tool such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Perplexity. The ARC describes doing so as a serious breach of confidentiality. Assessors may be asked about their AI use and must be transparent when requested. Where inappropriate use is suspected, the assessment may be removed from the process.

Researchers, applicants and reviewers must not include hidden prompts, invisible text, metadata instructions or other manipulative content intended to influence AI-assisted processing, review or assessment. Reviewers, assessors and staff involved in processing applications should be alert to the possibility that documents may contain hidden prompts designed to influence AI tools.

Other funder requirements#

For other funding bodies, industry partners and collaborative grants, key areas where AI requirements may apply include:

  • Disclosure of AI use in grant applications or progress reports
  • Restrictions on using AI tools to process data generated as part of the funded project
  • Intellectual property provisions that may affect ownership of AI-assisted outputs
  • Data sovereignty and storage requirements that may affect which AI tools can be used
  • Confidentiality requirements for peer review of grant applications

HDR candidates and honours students working on funded projects should discuss AI use with their supervisors and ensure that any planned use is consistent with the grant conditions. If in doubt, contact the Research Office for advice.

11. Publishing, Dissemination and Examination#

Publishers and journals#

Most major academic publishers now have explicit policies on the use of AI in submitted manuscripts, and these may differ from Charles Sturt’s institutional guidance. Before submitting a manuscript, conference paper, book chapter, grant application, dataset, code or image, researchers must check the relevant publisher, journal, conference, funder and collaborator requirements. Requirements vary and change over time.

  • AI tools cannot be listed as authors of academic publications.
  • Many publishers require disclosure of AI-assisted writing, image generation, data analysis or coding.
  • Some publishers prohibit AI-generated images or require additional permissions and labelling.
  • For ARC and NHMRC grant assessment requirements, including restrictions on AI use by assessors and peer reviewers, see Section 10.
  • Funders may have specific requirements for AI use in applications, assessment, data management, ethics or reporting.
  • Collaborative projects may have agreements that restrict uploading shared or partner data to third-party tools.

Thesis examiners and assessors#

Thesis examiners, peer reviewers, grant reviewers and assessment panel members must protect confidential and unpublished material. They should not upload theses, proposals, manuscripts, datasets, images, examination reports, reviewer comments or other confidential materials into public or commercial AI tools unless the relevant University, publisher, funder or review process explicitly permits this and the tool is approved for that purpose.

Where AI is permitted for review or examination support, reviewers should use it only in ways that preserve confidentiality, avoid bias and do not replace their expert judgement. Any material AI use in preparing reports should be declared if required by the relevant process.

12. Supervisory Conversations, Milestones and Honours Supervision#

AI use should be discussed early and revisited regularly. The supervisory relationship is an important site for navigating AI use in research. Supervisors and students are encouraged to have an explicit conversation about AI at the outset of the research project, and to revisit this conversation as the project evolves and as AI tools change.

Questions for students to consider#

  • What AI tools have I used, and for what purpose?
  • Did AI influence the research question, methods, analysis, interpretation or writing?
  • What records have I kept of my AI use?
  • How did I verify outputs?

Questions for supervisors to consider#

  • Is the proposed AI use appropriate for the discipline, project, data type and ethics approval?
  • Does it require disclosure, consent, a protocol amendment or data management change?
  • Have we documented our agreed position on AI use for this project?

Questions for panels or assessors to consider#

  • Has the student demonstrated independent knowledge of the work, methods and decisions?
  • Are declarations and records adequate?

Supervisors and assessors should not rely solely on AI-detection tools, which can be unreliable and may produce false positives or false negatives. Where there are concerns, process-oriented conversations, drafts, version histories, AI use logs, oral explanations and methodological questioning are usually more appropriate evidence of engagement.

For honours supervisors specifically#

Honours students may have different expectations about AI use based on their experience in coursework subjects. Be explicit about how your expectations for the thesis differ from those in coursework assessment contexts. Remind students that both course-level AI guidance and this research guidance apply to their work, and that where there is uncertainty, they should seek guidance before proceeding.

For HDR candidates, AI use should be addressed at candidature milestones, progress reviews and thesis preparation. Consider adding a prompt to milestone forms asking candidates to record whether and how AI has been used since the previous milestone.

13. Equity, Access and AI Literacy#

Access to AI tools is not equal. Differences in financial resources, hardware, internet connectivity, and digital literacy mean that some researchers have far greater access to GenAI capabilities than others. This creates potential inequities within and between research groups, institutions, and countries.

Charles Sturt University is committed to ensuring that all researchers have equitable access to the tools and support they need to engage with AI responsibly. Researchers and students who face barriers to accessing AI tools or AI literacy support are encouraged to:

  • Contact the Charles Sturt Library for guidance on available tools and resources
  • Speak with their supervisor about institutional access options, including the Charles Sturt provided enterprise version of Microsoft Copilot
  • Contact IT Services for advice on hardware and connectivity support

Supervisors should be mindful of equity when discussing AI use with their students and should not assume that all students have equivalent access to paid AI subscriptions or high-performance hardware.

Researchers should also be alert to equity considerations within their research itself. AI tools trained predominantly on data from wealthy, English-speaking, Western contexts may produce outputs that are less accurate, useful or fair when applied in other contexts. This is particularly relevant for research involving communities or populations that are underrepresented in AI training data.

AI Literacy in research#

AI literacy in research is not only the ability to use AI tools. It also involves understanding when AI use is appropriate, how AI outputs should be checked, and how AI may shape scholarly judgement, voice and interpretation. Researchers should develop AI literacy across three connected areas:

AI literacy areaWhat this means in research practice
Functional AI literacyKnowing what AI tools can and cannot do, when their use is appropriate or unnecessary, and how to verify and document AI-assisted work.
Critical AI literacyEvaluating AI outputs for accuracy, bias, omissions, embedded assumptions, overconfidence and ethical risk.
Rhetorical AI literacyMaintaining scholarly voice, disciplinary judgement, authorial agency and transparent attribution when AI contributes to writing, communication, translation, peer review or dissemination.

AI literacy should be understood as part of responsible scholarly practice, not simply as technical skill or tool familiarity.

15. Institutional Responsibilities#

Responsible AI use in research is a shared responsibility. While individual researchers remain accountable for their own research decisions, outputs and declarations, Charles Sturt University has an important role in supporting safe, ethical and effective AI use across the research environment.

The University should continue to support responsible AI use by providing clear guidance, training, advice pathways and access to approved tools where appropriate. This includes support for verifying AI outputs, protecting privacy and confidential information, managing copyright and intellectual property risks, addressing bias, supporting culturally safe research practice, and meeting ethics, data management, publisher and funder requirements.

Charles Sturt should also encourage a culture in which researchers can transparently disclose appropriate AI use without unnecessary stigma or fear of unfair penalty. Guidance should be reviewed regularly as tools, risks, laws, funder expectations and publisher requirements change. Where possible, the University should monitor common AI use cases and emerging issues to identify training needs, improve support, assess tool limitations, and promote proportionate and sustainable AI use.

Where AI tools are provided or recommended for research use, the University should clarify their approved use cases, privacy and security settings, data handling arrangements, limitations, and any restrictions on the use of confidential, unpublished, sensitive, participant, copyrighted, commercial-in-confidence or culturally restricted material.

Appendix A: Researcher AI Self-Check Before Use#

Before using an AI tool for research-related tasks, work through the following questions:

  • What task do I want AI to perform, and is AI appropriate for this task?
  • What data or content will I input, and do I have permission to use it this way?
  • Is the tool public, commercial, University-approved or otherwise protected?
  • Could the input include personal, sensitive, confidential, unpublished, restricted, copyrighted, partner-owned or culturally sensitive information?
  • Does the use align with ethics approval, participant consent, contracts, funder rules, publisher rules and University policy?
  • How will I verify the output?
  • Could the output introduce bias, stereotyping, exclusion, misinformation or fabricated evidence?
  • Do I need to record this use in an AI log?
  • Do I need to declare or acknowledge the use in a thesis, report, manuscript, grant application or presentation?
  • Have I read the user agreement for this tool? In particular: does the tool store, process or train on my inputs? Could inputs be accessed by third parties? Is data processed offshore? These questions are especially important before using any tool with unpublished or sensitive research material (Eacersall et al., 2025).
  • Who should I ask if uncertain?

Appendix E: AI Tool Suitability Checklist#

Use this checklist before adopting an AI tool for a research or review task, especially where the tool may influence methods, analysis, interpretation, reporting or evidence synthesis.

  • What is the tool intended to do?
  • What data sources, training data or databases does it rely on?
  • Has it been validated for this task, discipline, dataset or review type?
  • Is its performance good enough for the intended use?
  • Are its methods transparent and reproducible?
  • What human expertise is needed to use it safely?
  • What privacy, copyright, licensing, data storage or confidentiality issues apply?
  • Could errors, omissions or bias affect the findings or conclusions?

If the answer to any of these questions raises significant concerns, researchers should either avoid the tool or proceed only with documented mitigations, human verification and, where relevant, validation within the review. Criteria for acceptable performance should be considered before relying on AI outputs.

Appendix F: Examples of Responsible Practice#

The following case studies illustrate how the guidance may apply in common research contexts. They are examples only and do not replace ethics, copyright, privacy, funder, publisher or supervisory advice.

Case Study 1: Complementing, Not Replacing Academic Skills Scenario: Sarah, an HDR candidate studying ethnography, uses a GenAI tool to enhance her understanding of research methods. She asks the AI to generate an example of conducting field research in a hypothetical community to prepare for her fieldwork. How GenAI helps: The AI tool provides a scenario of observing cultural practices in a community, helping Sarah visualise her approach. She also inputs an overview of her research topic and asks for possible chapter structures. These suggestions are not accepted blindly but are refined according to her critical assessment of existing literature. Reflection: Sarah enhances her research preparation using AI as a complement, not a replacement. She remains responsible for critical thinking, creativity, and adapting AI insights to her unique research context.
Case Study 2: Safeguarding Data Privacy and Security Scenario: Alex is researching mental health interventions and uses GenAI to outline possible data analysis techniques. They remember that uploading raw interview transcripts into the tool might violate data privacy. How GenAI helps: Alex consults their supervisor and uses the GenAI tool to clarify thematic analysis methods, but avoids entering any confidential or identifiable data. Instead, they upload a fabricated dataset to experiment with analysis tools. For sensitive analysis, they use the CSU-approved enterprise version of Copilot. Reflection: Alex’s approach exemplifies responsible data handling by avoiding the input of confidential data into unsecured GenAI platforms. By using de-identified, synthetic data for initial testing and a secure, university-provided tool for subsequent analysis, Alex upheld both ethical and data protection standards.
Case Study 3: Practising Ethical Use and Integrity Scenario: Sophia, a PhD candidate in sociology, used GenAI tools to help refine her literature review, brainstorm new research directions, and identify gaps in existing studies. She realised that some sections of her thesis had been significantly influenced by the ideas generated through the AI tool. How GenAI helps: Sophia used GenAI to brainstorm research questions by inputting a broad theme and generating specific topics. She also used the tool to refine her thesis language and to identify gaps in existing research. She included a clear acknowledgement statement and cited the tools she used. Reflection: Sophia’s decision to acknowledge the AI’s influence, without presenting generated content as her own original work, exemplifies how researchers can ethically incorporate GenAI while maintaining integrity and compliance with research guidelines.
Case Study 4: Conducting Critical Evaluation of AI Outputs Scenario: Mark, an HDR candidate studying social media influence, uses GenAI to gather potential sources for his literature review. He notices that some references provided by the tool are fictitious. How GenAI helps: Mark uses the AI tool to identify key areas of research but double-checks every reference and fact against academic databases like Google Scholar and Scopus. In one instance, the AI incorrectly cites a journal article, prompting him to find the correct source manually. Reflection: Mark critically evaluates AI outputs, recognising that not all content is trustworthy. His attention to detail ensures his literature review is based on credible and verified sources.
Case Study 5: Upholding Inclusivity and Integrity Scenario: Emma, a researcher studying the portrayal of women in leadership roles, uses GenAI to draft a section of her thesis. Upon reviewing the AI-generated text, she observes that examples predominantly reference male CEOs, perpetuating a gendered stereotype of leadership. How GenAI helps: The AI tool provides quick insights into prevailing discourses. However, upon noticing the imbalance, Emma challenges these gendered representations and seeks additional scholarly resources, including studies on female leadership in non-Western contexts, to broaden the scope of her analysis. Reflection: Emma upholds inclusivity by actively identifying and correcting biased AI outputs. She ensures that her research amplifies underrepresented voices, addressing biases embedded in the AI’s training data.
Case Study 6: Literature Reviews Scenario: Priya, an honours student in public health, used a GenAI tool to get an overview of research on her topic. The tool produced a convincing-looking list of references, several of which turned out not to exist when she searched for them. How GenAI helps: Priya used the AI-generated overview to identify key themes and terminology in her field. She then conducted her own systematic search in Scopus and PubMed using those terms, verifying each source independently before including it in her review. Reflection: By using AI to orient herself in the field rather than to source her references, Priya avoided the risk of hallucinated citations while still benefiting from AI as a scoping tool.
Case Study 7: AI-Generated Images Scenario: James, a PhD candidate in environmental science, used an AI image generator to produce a conceptual diagram of an ecosystem for inclusion in a conference presentation. He later considered whether the same image could be used in his journal article. How GenAI helps: For the conference presentation, James disclosed the AI-generated origin of the diagram in his slide notes. For the journal article, he checked the journal’s author guidelines, which did not permit AI-generated figures. He recreated the diagram using a vector graphics tool based on his own research data. Reflection: Context determines what is appropriate. Disclosure and publisher compliance are non-negotiable. Checking publisher-specific policies before submission saves significant revision work later.
Case Study 8: Ongoing Learning and Adaptation Scenario: John is a communications researcher focusing on digital media trends. As AI tools continue to evolve, John stays updated on the latest advancements in NLP and attends AI-related workshops to integrate these advancements into his work. How GenAI helps: John uses NLP-based AI tools to analyse sentiment across social media platforms. He frequently adapts his research methodology based on the latest features in AI tools, ensuring his work remains current. He also engages in academic discussions on AI ethics and attends AI-focused conferences to stay informed. Reflection: John demonstrates a commitment to ongoing learning, continuously improving his research by incorporating the latest AI advancements. This adaptability ensures his research remains relevant in an evolving academic landscape.
Case Study 9: Human Research Ethics Scenario: Lena, an HDR candidate in education, received ethics approval to conduct interviews with teachers about their classroom practices. After data collection, she considered using an AI tool to assist with thematic coding of the transcripts. How GenAI helps: Lena reviewed her ethics application and noted that it did not specify AI-assisted analysis. She consulted her supervisor, who advised her to seek an ethics amendment before proceeding. She also chose to use the CSU-approved enterprise version of Copilot rather than a public tool, ensuring participant data remained within a protected environment. Reflection: Lena’s careful approach protected both her participants and the integrity of her research. Checking ethics obligations before introducing new analytical tools is essential.
Case Study 10: Publishing Scenario: David, a postdoctoral researcher, used an AI tool to help him refine the language of a manuscript he was preparing for submission to an international journal. He had also used an AI image generator to produce a conceptual figure. How GenAI helps: David checked the journal’s author guidelines, which required disclosure of AI-assisted language editing in the acknowledgements and prohibited AI-generated figures. He rewrote the figure using a graphics tool and added an appropriate acknowledgement of AI language assistance. Reflection: Checking publisher-specific policies before submission, not after, saved David from having to revise his manuscript at a later stage.
